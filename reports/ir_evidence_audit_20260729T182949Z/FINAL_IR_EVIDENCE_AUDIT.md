# Final IR Evidence Audit: Structure vs. Utility

**Purpose**: determine, from already-computed repository data only (no new API calls, no new judgments, no new algorithms), whether the evidence fully supports the revised IR thesis:

> Preference-graph construction materially affects graph structure, but structural consistency is not a reliable surrogate for downstream retrieval effectiveness.

All numbers below are read directly from `unified_configuration_results.csv`, `structure_utility_associations.csv`, `baseline_verification.csv`, and `cutoff_robustness.csv` in this directory, which were generated by `scripts/run_ir_evidence_audit.py` from the source tables cited throughout. No prior report or manuscript file was modified in producing this audit.

**A correction surfaced during this audit**: the n=419-query oracle-headroom analysis (`reports/repository_scale_headroom_analysis/`) belongs to the `negative_result_2026` paper track, not `JDIQ_2026` — `papers/JDIQ_2026/manuscript/main.tex` never references it. JDIQ's own backbone evidence is `reports/full_calibrated_core/outputs/calibrated_all4/paper_package/tables/`. Both tracks are used below and tagged by `paper_track` in the unified table.

**Two real bugs were caught and fixed while building this audit** (documented here for transparency, since they materially affect trust in the output): (1) a column named `holm_active_ms1_family` in `pool_cutoff_statistics.csv` is a Holm-adjusted **p-value** (float), not a boolean, despite its name — comparing it with `== True` silently matched rows where the p-value happened to equal exactly 1.0 (pandas casts `True`→`1.0`), producing a spurious "24/216 Holm-significant" result that contradicted the manuscript's documented "0/110." Fixed by filtering to non-null rows and comparing `< 0.05`, which reproduces the manuscript's 0/110 exactly (0/40 @nDCG5, 0/35 @nDCG10, 0/35 @nDCG20). (2) An unrelated pandas attribute-access bug (`df.holm_significant_at_0.05` parses as `df.holm_significant_at_0` followed by the float literal `.05`, not as a single column name) was caught before it could silently return wrong values; fixed with bracket indexing (`df["holm_significant_at_0.05"]`).

## 3. Structure vs. utility: association analysis

`structure_utility_associations.csv` (7 rows), computed at two granularities:

**Aggregated (dataset × protocol × regime), n=12** — JDIQ's own structure and repair-effect tables, joined on their shared key:

| Feature | Pearson r | Pearson p | Spearman ρ | Spearman p |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| `cyclic_query_pct` | 0.175 | 0.586 | 0.399 | 0.199 |
| `mean_largest_scc` | 0.142 | 0.660 | 0.399 | 0.199 |
| `mean_fas_weight_removed` | 0.204 | 0.524 | 0.406 | 0.190 |
| `mean_normalized_fas_weight_removed` | 0.184 | 0.567 | 0.391 | 0.209 |
| `graph_density` | 0.403 | 0.193 | 0.609 | 0.036 |

None of the five Pearson correlations reach significance (all p > 0.19); `graph_density`'s Spearman ρ=0.609 is nominally significant (p=0.036) but is a single test among five at n=12 — **not Holm-corrected here and not treated as a finding**; flagged explicitly as exploratory. A finer-grained per-dataset sign-consistency check (the Simpson's-paradox screen) could not be run at this aggregation level because each dataset only contributes 3 regime rows (below the n≥5 threshold used); this is a genuine, stated limitation of the *aggregated* view, not of the evidence base as a whole (see the per-query check below, which does not have this limitation).

**Per-query row-level (larger-pool study), n=17,532** — `removed_weight_fraction` vs. `delta_ndcg`, every query × pool_size × cutoff config:

- Pooled: Pearson r=0.046 (p=1.3×10⁻⁹), Spearman ρ=0.044 (p=6.5×10⁻⁹).
- Within-query (Fisher-z average across 179 queries with ≥5 distinct configs each): r=0.055.

**Interpretation, explicitly labeled non-causal**: the pooled correlation is *statistically* significant only because n is enormous (17,532 pseudo-replicated rows from repeated queries) — the effect size (r≈0.05) is practically negligible, explaining under 0.3% of variance. The within-query estimate (0.055) is close in sign and magnitude to the pooled estimate (0.046), which is itself evidence *against* a Simpson's-paradox-style aggregation artifact at this granularity: pooling repeated queries does not appear to be manufacturing or hiding a real relationship here. Both estimates agree: more structural repair activity is associated with **at most a negligible, not a meaningful,** retrieval change.

## 4. Publication table and figure

- **Table**: `tables/publication_table_structure_vs_utility.csv` / `.md` — one row per (dataset, regime), reporting fraction cyclic, mean largest SCC, mean normalized repair objective, mean ΔnDCG@10, and helped/harmed/unchanged counts, from the JDIQ backbone's primary protocol.
- **Figure**: `figures/structure_vs_utility.png` (source: `figures/structure_vs_utility_SOURCE.py`) — two panels: (a) per-query repair-objective vs. ΔnDCG@10 scatter (larger-pool study, n≈17.5k, colored by dataset, pooled linear fit ≈ flat at zero); (b) aggregated cyclicity vs. mean ΔnDCG@10 by dataset×regime, with the ±0.01 meaningful-threshold band marked.

**Aggregation-artifact warning, stated explicitly because the figure itself reveals it**: in panel (b), the two points with by far the largest *magnitude* effect are both `ms1` (high-cyclicity) configurations — one moderately positive (~+0.0015) and one that is the single most negative point in the whole panel (~−0.014, exceeding the harm threshold). This is a real, visible asymmetry: the highest-cyclicity regime is *not* uniformly near-zero, it is the *most variable*, in both directions. Do not describe panel (b) as "everything clusters at zero" — the correct, more precise statement is "the *aggregate* effect is negligible and centered near zero across the cyclicity range, but the *variance* of the effect is highest exactly where cyclicity is highest, and that variance includes the one clearly-harmful cell in the whole backbone." This nuance strengthens, not weakens, the thesis (structure is not a reliable proxy — including not reliably predicting the *sign* of the retrieval effect, even when it does predict where the effect will be largest in magnitude).

## 7. Proposed manuscript text

### Proposed Results subsection

> **Structural repair activity does not track retrieval effect, at any cutoff or aggregation level.** Across the primary construction protocol, cyclicity ranges from near-zero to over 99% of queries depending on normalization and pooling choice, yet the correlation between structural repair activity (fraction of graph weight removed) and the resulting change in nDCG is negligible at every granularity we tested: r=0.046 pooled across 17,532 per-query configurations (statistically distinguishable from zero only because of the large, pseudo-replicated sample; practically explaining under 0.3% of variance), and r=0.055 when averaged within-query to remove the pseudo-replication concern. At the aggregated dataset×regime level (n=12), none of five candidate structural predictors (cyclicity, largest SCC, raw or normalized repair objective, graph density) reaches significance. Zero of 110 larger-pool nDCG cells survive Holm correction at any of nDCG@5, @10, or @20, and this null is corroborated by an independent real-dataset exact-vs-greedy repair comparison across nDCG@5/@10/@20, MAP, and MRR (all |mean delta| ≤ 0.001). A repository-scale, 419-query oracle-headroom analysis places an upper bound on what *any* selective repair policy could achieve: 0.0025 (95% CI [0.0020, 0.0030]), roughly eight times below the study's own minimum-detectable-effect floor (0.0207). Three independent follow-up studies using real multi-provider LLM-judge preference graphs (n=120 query-graphs; Azure, Gemini, Cohere, Fireworks; SciDocs, FiQA) reproduce the same qualitative result outside the classical multi-ranker setting: a substantially richer repair-candidate space recovers no meaningful headroom (0.0054, CI [0.0029, 0.0084]); none of eight alternative graph-to-ranking extraction methods (including a new HodgeRank-based extractor) shows a practically meaningful average gain, and the method most associated with occasional wins in an oracle sense (Borda) is in fact significantly *worse* than the incumbent on average (−0.0080, CI [−0.0139, −0.0033]); and repair's rare benefit is not predictable from any of nine categories of pre-repair graph features, with repair mildly harmful on average (mean-delta CI entirely below zero) and beneficial on only 1 of 120 evaluated query-graphs.

### Proposed Discussion subsection

> These results converge across two independent graph-construction mechanisms (classical multi-ranker score fusion and real multi-provider LLM pairwise judging), three independent remediation strategies (whole-graph repair, a substantially richer repair-candidate space, and alternative extraction methods), and every cutoff and metric we evaluated (nDCG@5/@10/@20, MAP, MRR). The consistency of this null — not any single negative result — is the paper's evidence that the structure-utility gap is a property of the retrieval problem as tested, not an artifact of one repair algorithm, one extraction method, or one dataset. Simple graph-free baselines already in common use (CombSUM, RRF) already match or exceed every graph-based method we evaluated, including every repaired variant, at the primary protocol; practitioners building preference-graph pipelines for retrieval should not expect structural consistency repair, richer repair strategies, or extraction-method substitution to reliably improve effectiveness under the conditions we tested (pool sizes 6-50, nDCG-family metrics, the datasets and construction rules specified in §7 of the audit). We caution against over-generalizing the one nominally-significant aggregated correlation (graph density, Spearman p=0.036, n=12, uncorrected for the five structural features tested) — it does not survive a multiple-testing correction and is not reproduced at the much larger per-query granularity, where the corresponding pooled and within-query estimates both indicate a negligible relationship.
