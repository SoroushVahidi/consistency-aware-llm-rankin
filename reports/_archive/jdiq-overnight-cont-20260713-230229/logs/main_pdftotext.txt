Score Normalization and Vote Construction Govern Preference-Graph Repair
Outcomes in Multi-Ranker Retrieval
ANONYMOUS AUTHOR(S)
Preference-graph repair is often motivated by the idea that removing cyclic inconsistency should improve downstream ranking
quality, but that conclusion depends on how the graph is constructed. We study this question on SciDocs, FiQA, HotpotQA, and

9

BRIGHT using stored BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM candidate scores, fixed candidate pools, and public qrels. Our primary analysis

10

uses a normalized retention-matched protocol: per-query, per-ranker min–max normalization followed by retention-matched vote

11

and edge thresholds. As a raw-score ablation, we also evaluate the unnormalized construction that treats native heterogeneous score

12

margins as directly comparable. That unnormalized construction is severely scale dominated: when BM25 participated in an edge, its

13

conditional mean weight share was 0.988, versus 0.512 under the primary protocol. Normalization and vote construction materially

14

changed retained votes, edge weights, cyclicity, removed edges, and several repaired-versus-unrepaired signs. Under the primary

15
16

protocol, repair still changes graph structure, but structural consistency and retrieval effectiveness remain distinct: repair can reduce

17

graph inconsistency without producing a reliable improvement in ranked retrieval. No repaired-versus- unrepaired improvement

18

remains robust after permutation testing, bootstrap uncertainty, influence analysis, and multiplicity correction. Simple baselines such

19

as reciprocal rank fusion and CombSUM also remain strong comparators under the normalized graphs. The conclusion is therefore

20

methodological as well as empirical: score normalization and vote construction jointly determine the preference graph on which

21

repair operates, and claims about repair effectiveness must report those choices explicitly rather than treating graph construction as

22

a neutral preprocessing step.

INTRODUCTION

35

Modern retrieval pipelines rarely rely on a single ranking signal. Sparse retrievers, dense retrievers, and other learned

36

ranking components are often treated as separate sources of evidence whose outputs must be reconciled rather than

37
38

trusted individually [1, 3, 6, 14]. Preference graphs offer a natural way to preserve that disagreement: for a given query, a

39

directed edge from document 𝑢 to document 𝑣 records evidence that 𝑢 should outrank 𝑣, and the edge weight determines

40

how strongly that preference should influence the final ordering. This representation is appealing because it makes

41

conflict explicit instead of collapsing it immediately into a single scalar score. But the graph is only as meaningful as

42
43
44
45
46
47
48
49
50

the procedure used to construct it. If heterogeneous ranker scores are converted into edge weights without a defensible
notion of comparability, then the resulting graph can reflect score scale as much as evidential quality.

That issue matters because preference-graph repair operates entirely on the constructed graph. Once weighted pair-

54

wise evidence is aggregated, the graph may contain directed cycles, so no single ranking can satisfy every recorded

55
56

preference. A standard response is to repair the graph by removing a minimum-weight feedback arc set before ex-

57

tracting a final ranking [1, 12]. This intervention improves structural consistency relative to the graph’s own edge

58

weights, but structural consistency and retrieval effectiveness are not the same objective. A repair step can remove

59

cycle-inducing edges, reduce a graph-internal inconsistency measure, and still fail to improve a relevance metric such

60
61

as normalized discounted cumulative gain (nDCG) if the modified edges do not alter the final ranking in a helpful

62

way. For that reason, a study of preference-graph repair must examine not only the repair algorithm but also the

63

graph-construction choices that determine what the repair sees.

64
65
66
67

This paper studies that upstream dependency directly. Rather than asking for a predictive answer to “when repair
helps,” we examine how score normalization and vote construction govern the preference graph on which repair operates, and how those choices affect both structural outcomes and downstream retrieval behavior. We evaluate four

68

benchmarks—SciDocs, FiQA, HotpotQA, and BRIGHT—with stored BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM score files [4, 15, 19, 23].

69

Our primary protocol applies per-query, per-ranker min–max normalization followed by retention-matched thresh-

70
71

olding; we retain the unnormalized raw-margin construction only as an ablation. The motivation for this comparison

72

is empirical, not cosmetic. Under the raw-margin construction, BM25 contributed nearly all aggregate edge weight

73

whenever it participated, so the graph was largely BM25-weighted even when three rankers were nominally present.

74

Treating such raw heterogeneous margins as directly comparable support signals is not a neutral preprocessing choice;

75
76

it is a substantive graph-construction assumption that shapes every downstream result.

77

Once graph construction is treated as a first-class variable, several measured outcomes change. Normalization and

78

vote construction materially alter retained votes, edge weights, cyclicity, removed edges, and some repaired-versus-

79

unrepaired signs. They also sharpen the distinction between total cyclicity and the smaller amount of nontrivial cyclic-

80
81

ity that remains after direct mutual pairs are deleted. Repair therefore remains structurally active under the normalized

82

graphs, but its retrieval effects are method-dependent and construction-sensitive rather than stable properties of “re-

83

pair” in the abstract. The central empirical result is not that one dataset provides a robust positive exception, nor that

84

repair has a construction-independent null effect. It is that graph-construction choices substantially determine both

85
86

the structure being repaired and the retrieval conclusions drawn from that repair.

87

This framing changes how the paper’s contributions should be understood. We do not present feedback-arc-set repair

88

as a generally superior reranking strategy, and we do not interpret raw-margin results as primary evidence. Instead,

89

the paper contributes a normalized evaluation protocol for multi-ranker preference graphs, a structural analysis that

90
91

separates mutual pairs from longer-cycle behavior, a four-dataset assessment of repaired versus unrepaired retrieval

92

effects under paired statistical testing and robustness checks, and a raw-versus-normalized ablation showing that some

93

repaired- versus-unrepaired signs are not stable under defensible graph reconstruction. These contributions support a

94

more limited but more defensible conclusion: preference-graph repair outcomes are not invariant to score normaliza-

95
96
97

tion and vote construction, and structural consistency should not be assumed to translate into retrieval improvement
without direct evaluation.

98

BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

110

Preference-graph methods sit at the intersection of several literatures: rank aggregation, noisy pairwise-comparison

111

ranking, retrieval fusion, and data quality for derived analytical artifacts. The goal of this paper is narrower than a

112
113

new ranking-method contribution. We do not introduce a new feedback-arc-set solver, a new retrieval ranker, or a

114

new fusion rule. Instead, we study how heterogeneous ranker scores are converted into weighted preference graphs

115

and how those graph-construction choices govern the measured effect of repair on both structural consistency and

116

downstream retrieval.

117
118
119

Rank aggregation and preference graphs. Preference graphs are a standard representation for aggregating pairwise evidence across voters, rankers, or judges [1, 6, 17]. For a query-specific candidate set, a directed edge 𝑢 → 𝑣

120

records support for ranking document 𝑢 ahead of document 𝑣, while the edge weight records the strength of that sup-

121

port. This representation is useful because it preserves disagreement as explicit graph structure instead of collapsing

122
123

it immediately into a single fused score.

124

Feedback arc set repair and Kemeny-style consistency. Once a preference graph contains a directed cycle, no

125

total ranking can satisfy every recorded preference simultaneously. Kemeny’s classical consensus ranking minimizes

126

total pairwise disagreement with input preferences [11]; deciding an optimal Kemeny ranking and computing a min-

127
128

imum feedback arc set are closely related combinatorial problems [1, 6]. Weighted feedback arc set is NP-hard [10],

129

so algorithms either optimize exact formulations on moderate instances or use heuristics. Classical work therefore

130

studies minimum weighted feedback arc set (MWFAS) and Kemeny-style objectives that remove or violate as little

131

edge weight as possible to obtain an acyclic ordering [1, 6, 8, 12]. That optimization target is purely graph-internal.

132
133
134

It formalizes structural consistency, not relevance to external retrieval judgments. This distinction is central here: a
better solution to the graph’s own repair objective need not improve a metric such as nDCG.

135

Probabilistic ranking from noisy pairwise preferences. Classical Bradley–Terry models treat pairwise out-

136

comes as noisy comparisons about latent item strengths [2]. Rank Centrality likewise models pairwise outcomes as

137
138

noisy evidence about a latent ordering and derives node scores from the induced comparison graph [17]. Those proba-

139

bilistic methods are useful here as graph-dependent baselines; graph-repair methods instead remove edges to enforce

140

acyclicity rather than fitting a probabilistic ranking model. Both families share the same upstream dependence: the

141

object they consume is the constructed preference graph, so changing vote extraction or edge weighting can alter their

142
143

behavior even when the upstream retrieval runs are unchanged.

144

Rank fusion in information retrieval. Information-retrieval systems already combine heterogeneous signals

145

through score or rank fusion, including reciprocal rank fusion (RRF), CombSUM, and Borda-style methods [5, 9, 13].

146

Analyses of multiple-evidence combination clarify how score- and rank-based fusion behave when several retrieval

147
148

strategies contribute overlapping evidence [13]. These baselines matter here for two reasons. First, they provide graph-

149

free comparators against which repaired and unrepaired graph methods must be judged. Second, they make explicit

150

a design choice that preference-graph work can hide: when heterogeneous retrieval scores are merged, some form of

151

scale handling or an explicit decision to ignore cross-model score comparability becomes part of the method before

152
153

score magnitudes are interpreted jointly. We keep CombSUM as the score-summation baseline and do not expand the

154

primary baseline family with CombMNZ; the study’s comparative focus is repair sensitivity under fixed pools rather

155

than an exhaustive fusion bake-off.

156

157

Score normalization for heterogeneous retrieval signals. BM25, TF-IDF, and neural similarity models operate

158

on different native score scales. A raw difference of 0.05 has no reason to represent comparable evidential strength

159
160

across those models, a point long recognized in meta-search score-normalization work [16]. The methodological issue

161

studied in this paper is therefore not whether preference graphs are useful in principle, but whether weighted graph

162

construction is stable when native heterogeneous score margins are treated as if they were directly comparable. Our

163
164
165

primary normalized protocol makes that assumption explicit and testable rather than leaving it implicit in the edge
weights.

166

LLM pairwise ranking and inconsistency. Recent work has used large language models as retrieval judges,

167

rerankers, and pairwise preference sources [18, 20]. That line of work makes inconsistency salient, but it does not

168

remove the measurement problem studied here. Whether the pairwise evidence comes from classical rankers or from

169
170
171

LLM judgments, downstream claims about “repair” still depend on how pairwise evidence is filtered, weighted, and
aggregated into a graph.

172

Derived-artifact data quality. We treat the preference graph itself as a derived data artifact whose properties

173

can be audited. Cyclicity, strongly connected components, mutual contradictions, and removed feedback-arc weight

174
175

are intrinsic properties of the graph produced by a construction pipeline, whereas retrieval metrics such as nDCG are

176

extrinsic properties of rankings evaluated against qrels. The contribution of this paper is to separate those two measure-

177

ment families and show that conclusions about repair are not invariant to score normalization and vote construction.

178

In this sense, the paper is a study of graph construction and measurement sensitivity, not a claim that a particular

179
180
181
182
183
184
185
186

repair heuristic solves retrieval reranking.

This section defines the calibrated preference-graph pipeline used for the primary analysis. The order matters. Upstream
score scale affects pairwise margins; pairwise margins affect retained votes; retained votes determine weighted graph
edges; graph structure determines what repair sees; and repair influences ranking extraction only through the modified

graph. Table 1 summarizes notation used in this section and Section 4, and Figure 1 summarizes the end-to-end pipeline

3.1 Upstream Scores and Candidate Pool

192

The primary pipeline starts from stored score files for three upstream rankers: BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM. For each

193

query 𝑞, we first define a shared candidate pool 𝐷𝑞 from the union of documents scored by at least one ranker. If that

194
195

union already has size at most the dataset-specific pool size, we keep all documents. Otherwise we rank the union

196

197

candidates. Candidate pooling therefore uses an RRF-family computation, but it is distinct from both the Prior ranking

198

and the reported RRF baseline because it is applied before graph construction and before candidate restriction.

Protocol Audit of Stored LLM Judgments

We also inspected stored real-LLM judgment corpora to determine whether they provide an independent validation

1169

of the calibrated mechanical study. They do not currently support that role. The stored corpora exhibit parser defaults

1170

that silently map ambiguous or malformed outputs to a fixed label, measurable provider-dependent position bias,

1171

incomplete raw-text and exclusion logging, and protocol differences across providers and method families. These issues

1172

are serious enough that apparent cyclicity in a naively constructed LLM-derived graph can be dominated by pipeline

1173
1174

artifacts rather than by genuine model disagreement.

1175

Quantitatively, a stored pairwise-judgment corpus collected from the Cohere and Azure OpenAI APIs on FiQA,

1176

HotpotQA, and BRIGHT shows the following. The shared response parser silently defaults ambiguous or malformed

1177

outputs to a fixed label on 1.1% of 12,020 responses with preserved raw text (0.0% on FiQA for both providers; up to 3.8%

1178
1179

on HotpotQA for Cohere). Among well-formed, unambiguous responses, both providers show statistically significant

1180

position bias, but in opposite directions: Azure prefers the first-shown candidate 53–58% of the time across the three

1181

datasets, while Cohere prefers the second-shown candidate 61–70% of the time (exact binomial 𝑝 < 0.01 in every

1182

provider–dataset cell); pooling the two providers would largely cancel this out and hide both effects. Forward and

1183
1184

reverse presentations of the same candidate pair agree with each other only 59–85% of the time depending on provider

1185

and dataset (Cohen’s 𝜅 0.27–0.71, fair to substantial). In a preference graph built directly from these judgments (not the

1186

mechanical graph used elsewhere in this paper), removing edges produced by the parser’s default-label rule and by the

1187

1188
1189

to 0–5%), indicating that most of that graph’s apparent cyclicity reflects these pipeline defaults rather than genuine

1190

1191

1192

DISCUSSION

1222

Sections 5–7 support a conclusion more specific than a naive “repair either helps or it does not” framing: preference-

1223

graph repair cannot be interpreted independently of the score-normalization and vote-construction protocol that cre-

1224
1225

ates the graph. Under the primary normalized retention-matched protocol, repair still changes graph structure, but its

1226

downstream retrieval effect remains construction-sensitive, method-dependent, and not robustly positive across the

1227

tested benchmarks.

1228
1229
1230
1231

Normalization is part of the model, not preprocessing trivia. The preference graph inherits whatever score
scale is used to construct pairwise margins. Under the unnormalized raw-margin construction, those margins were
not comparable across BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM, and BM25 contributed almost all conditional edge weight when

1232

it participated. The primary normalized protocol substantially reduced that scale domination and, with it, changed

1233

which votes survived, which edges were retained, and which edges were removed during repair. The practical lesson is

1234
1235

methodological: conclusions about graph repair are inseparable from the normalization and threshold semantics used

1236

upstream. We do not claim that per-query, per-ranker min–max normalization is universally optimal, only that it is

1237

the best-supported primary protocol in this study because it materially reduced score-scale distortion and enabled a

1238

controlled comparison against the raw ablation.

1239
1240

Structural success does not guarantee retrieval gain. FAS repair (Eq. (9)) optimizes a graph-internal objective,

1241

whereas nDCG evaluates ranked retrieval against qrels. That distinction matters empirically: the calibrated experi-

1242

ments still show nonzero repair activity in the cyclic regimes, yet structural improvement does not imply a better

1243

final ranking. Depending on the query, extraction rule, and fusion stage, a repaired graph can be retrieval-inactive,

1244
1245

evaluation-invisible, neutral, or harmful. The qrels-anchored diagnostics in Section 5 are useful for interpretation, but

1246

they should not be treated as independent validation of the graph objective itself. Structural consistency and retrieval

1247

effectiveness remain related but distinct empirical targets.

1248

Manuscript submitted to ACM

Score Normalization and Vote Construction Govern Preference-Graph Repair Outcomes in Multi-Ranker Retrieval 25
1249

Mutual contradictions and longer-cycle structure should be reported separately. The calibrated results also

1250

change how cyclicity should be read. Apparent graph cyclicity is substantial under the primary protocol before mutual

1251
1252

pair deletion, but much of it arises from direct bidirectional contradictions rather than from longer cyclic structure.

1253

After deleting mutual pairs, the mean cyclic-query rate drops markedly, leaving a smaller but still nonzero residual of

1254

nontrivial cyclicity. This does not make the remaining cycles unimportant, but it does mean that total ms1 cyclicity

1255

should not be interpreted automatically as genuine Condorcet-style nontransitivity. Future work should report both

1256
1257

direct mutual contradiction and post-deletion cyclicity by default.

1258

Repair effects are method- and calibration-dependent. The same repaired graph does not propagate uniformly

1259

through Copeland, Markov, balance, and the hybrid variants. Some calibrated cells change sign relative to the raw pack-

1260

age, and the balance-family zero-delta pattern in this study reflects propagation failure through the ranking pipeline

1261
1262

in this study, not a general impossibility result for balance-based repair. The hybrid results similarly show that fu-

1263

sion can either dampen noisy graph changes or suppress potentially meaningful ones, depending on how much signal

1264

survives calibration and how strongly the prior ranking dominates the final score. Positive point estimates therefore

1265

require more than a favorable mean: in this study, none remains robust once paired testing, influence analysis, and

1266
1267

multiplicity correction are applied together. HotpotQA is the clearest example of a positive-looking but non-robust

1268

cell: its calibrated ms1 Copeland-hybrid mean is positive, yet the effect is concentrated in a few influential queries, the

1269

paired permutation test is not compelling, and the cell does not survive Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment.

1270
1271
1272

Simple baselines remain important under calibrated graphs. The present study also does not support graph
repair as a superior default retrieval strategy. Reciprocal-rank fusion and CombSUM remain strong comparators under

1273

the calibrated protocol, and the prior ranking continues to absorb much of the useful signal in the hybrids. At the same

1274

time, these comparisons should be read with the candidate-pool design in mind: the experiments are centered on an

1275

RRF-family pooling scheme, and Prior and the RRF baseline, although related, are not interchangeable rows. The right

1276
1277
1278

takeaway is therefore cautious rather than triumphalist: simple fusion baselines remain strong under the calibrated
protocol, and graph-dependent methods must justify their added complexity on a paired, dataset-specific basis.

1279

Implications for future preference-graph studies. Preference-graph experiments should report calibration,

1280

threshold interpretation, common-query pairing, mutual-pair prevalence, residual nontrivial cyclicity, repair objec-

1281
1282
1283

tive, ranking-extraction method, influence diagnostics, and multiplicity correction as first-class design choices rather
than hidden preprocessing details. Raw-score constructions can still be informative as ablations, but not as primary

1284

evidence when the underlying rankers operate on heterogeneous score scales. This paper therefore does not offer a

1285

validated predictive selector for when repair will help. It offers a narrower but more defensible conclusion: repair out-

1286
1287
1288

comes depend materially on the graph-construction process, and any future claim about repair effectiveness should
make that dependence explicit.

LIMITATIONS

This study’s limitations fall into several categories, detailed below.

1294

Normalization remains a design choice, not a proven optimum. The primary protocol uses per-query, per-

1295

ranker min–max normalization with retention-matched thresholds because that combination substantially reduced

1296

score-scale domination and supported the most defensible controlled comparison in this study. It is not the only plau-

1297

sible normalization. Other transformations in the pilot changed graph structure and some downstream signs, so the

1298
1299
1300

present paper demonstrates construction sensitivity rather than proving that one normalization is universally best.
Manuscript submitted to ACM

26

Anon.

1301

Candidate pooling and baseline roles are RRF-centered. The experiments use an RRF-family candidate-pool

1302

construction and then compare graph methods against Prior, RRF, CombSUM, Borda, and related variants within that

1303
1304

setting. Because candidate selection, Prior, and the RRF baseline occupy different implementation roles, conclusions

1305

may differ under independently constructed pools or different prior rankings. The paper documents these distinctions,

1306

but it does not establish pool-invariant behavior.

1307
1308
1309

Nontrivial cyclicity is smaller than headline cyclicity. Under the primary protocol, cyclicity after removing
direct mutual pairs is materially lower than cyclicity before deletion. That means some regimes offer limited opportu-

1310

nity for repair to act on longer-cycle structure, and the paper does not establish how the same methods would behave

1311

on corpora with naturally high nontrivial cyclicity.

1312
1313
1314

Retention-target sensitivity changes structure more than retrieval robustness. The retention-matching procedure (Section 3.3) is an experimental control for comparing raw and normalized graph construction at approxi-

1315

mately matched retention, not a universally optimal threshold-selection method. A sensitivity sweep of alternative

1316

retention/threshold policies shows that graph density, cyclicity, and removed-edge patterns change materially across

1317

policies, and some repaired-versus-unrepaired cell signs flip. Across those policies, however, no positive repaired-

1318
1319

versus-unrepaired nDCG cell survives Holm correction. We therefore retain raw-reference retention matching as the

1320

primary protocol and interpret structural diagnostics as policy-sensitive, while the main multiplicity-corrected re-

1321

trieval conclusion remains robust under the tested alternatives.

1322
1323
1324

The repair algorithm is heuristic and order-sensitive. Our main repair procedure is greedy cycle peeling with
no approximation guarantee, and this is the only repair used to produce the primary evidence in Sections 5–6. As a

1325

robustness check (Section 4.4), we additionally solved the exact minimum-weight feedback arc set problem with an

1326

open-source ILP solver on the complete 1,025-query primary analysis: the exact solution differs from greedy’s on most

1327

cyclic queries and removes materially less weight, but this does not change which repaired-versus-unrepaired retrieval

1328
1329

cells survive multiplicity correction. In principle, cycle-discovery order, insertion order, and alternative optimization

1330

strategies could still produce different greedy-repaired graphs and therefore different downstream rankings; the exact-

1331

solver check bounds, but does not eliminate, this concern for the primary greedy results.

1332
1333
1334

Statistical power and influence remain limiting factors. Several cells have modest query counts, and some
positive-looking means are concentrated in a small number of influential queries. The paper addresses this with paired

1335

permutation tests, bootstrap intervals, leave-one-out analysis, influence removal, and multiplicity correction, but those

1336

diagnostics also reinforce the main caution: no positive repaired-versus-unrepaired effect remains robust after Holm

1337

or Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

1338
1339

Metric scope is narrower than graph scope. nDCG is the main retrieval metric, and its cutoff determines whether

1340

some ranking changes are visible at all. MAP and MRR are reported only as secondary robustness checks and are not

1341

part of the primary multiplicity-corrected nDCG family; pool-truncated Recall@𝑘 is not used as a confirmatory metric.

1342

The qrels themselves are incomplete and dataset-dependent, and BEW/PIC are computed from the same relevance

1343
1344
1345

evidence rather than from an independent source of graph truth. The paper therefore does not claim that improved
graph consistency implies a general improvement in user utility beyond the measured retrieval setting.

1346

The stored LLM records are limited as evidence. As Section 7.2 now makes explicit, parser defaults, position

1347

bias, incomplete logging, and protocol differences prevent the current LLM artifacts from supporting a clean claim

1348
1349

about genuine model nontransitivity or from serving as independent validation of the calibrated mechanical study.

1350

External validity remains bounded. The primary evidence is drawn from four retrieval benchmarks, three me-

1351

chanical rankers, and a specific set of vote regimes, extraction methods, and fusion choices. We do not claim that the

CONCLUSION

1361

This study examines not only whether preference-graph repair affects retrieval, but also whether that conclusion is

1362

stable under defensible choices for normalizing and aggregating heterogeneous ranking scores. The central finding is

1363

methodological. Raw heterogeneous ranker margins severely distorted weighted graph construction, producing near-

1364

total BM25 dominance whenever BM25 participated. Replacing those raw margins with per-query, per-ranker min–

1365
1366

max normalization and retention-matched thresholds materially changed retained edges, cyclicity, removed edges, and

1367

several repaired-versus-unrepaired signs.

1368

Under this primary normalized protocol, repair still improves internal graph consistency in the active regimes, but

1369

structural consistency and retrieval effectiveness do not move together reliably. Positive repaired-versus-unrepaired

1370
1371

point estimates remain in some cells, yet none survives the combined scrutiny of paired testing, influence analysis,

1372

and multiplicity correction. The paper therefore does not support a construction-invariant claim that repair helps, nor

1373

does it support a single robust positive exception. It instead shows that repair outcomes are sensitive to normalization

1374

and vote construction, and that simple fusion baselines remain strong comparators under the normalized protocol.

1375
1376

The practical implication is modest but important: preference-graph repair should be evaluated together with the

1377

score normalization, threshold semantics, mutual-pair handling, and ranking-extraction choices that determine the

1378

graph on which repair operates. Future work may yet find settings where repair yields a robust retrieval gain, but this

1379

study shows that such a claim cannot be treated as independent of graph construction.
