  Consistency Does Not Reliably Predict Retrieval Utility: An
  Exact-and-Heuristic Audit of Preference-Graph Repair for Multi-Ranker
  Retrieval}

\abstract{\textbf{Purpose:} Preference-graph repair is often treated as if
restoring acyclicity improves ranking quality. Heuristic studies leave
greedy graph-objective suboptimality open for null results, and
construction is rarely experimental. This study tests whether heuristic
or exact minimum-weight feedback-arc-set (MWFAS) edge-deletion repair
yields a multiplicity-corrected retrieval gain for score-derived
multi-ranker graphs.

\textbf{Methods:} On four public benchmarks, graphs were built from
stored BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM scores under three vote-construction
regimes. Unrepaired, greedy, and exact repairs were compared with paired
sign-flip tests and Holm correction. Exact SCIP diagnosed greedy
suboptimality for the stated edge-deletion objective.

\textbf{Results:} Construction dominated structure: a conservative
regime was always acyclic; a permissive regime was cyclic for most
queries. Normalization cut BM25 edge-weight share from $0.988$ to
$0.512$. Repair removed contradictory weight and changed top-$k$
membership by $10.6\%$ when pools exceeded the cutoff. No
repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG comparison survived Holm correction in
the canonical, larger-pool, or exact-repair families. Dataset-macro
nDCG kept CombSUM competitive ($0.554$) with the best repaired hybrid
($0.546$). Exact repair removed less weight than greedy yet yielded no
corrected retrieval gain.

\textbf{Conclusion:} Under this protocol, repair is structurally active
but not a validated nDCG optimization. Missing Holm-corrected evidence
is not equivalence; smaller effects remain possible. The contribution
is a factorized audit of construction, repair, extraction, and utility,
with exact MWFAS only as an identification control.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Pairwise preferences are a natural form of ranking evidence: a judgment
states only that one document should be ranked ahead of another for a given
query. Such evidence arises from multiple ranking signals (lexical and dense
retrievers, cross-encoders, or large language models) or from systems that
elicit pairwise comparisons. Aggregating these judgments yields a
query-specific weighted directed preference graph, where an edge
$u \rightarrow v$ encodes evidence for ranking $u$ ahead of $v$.

This representation preserves disagreement, but it is structurally fragile.
Aggregating imperfectly correlated sources can produce directed cycles
($u \rightarrow v \rightarrow w \rightarrow u$), so that no total order is
consistent with all retained edges. Restoring acyclicity by removing a
minimum-weight set of conflicting edges is therefore a plausible
preprocessing step before ranking extraction: a self-consistent graph seems
a better input for any subsequent scoring rule.

That intuition conflates four stages that must be kept separate.
\emph{Construction} chooses which votes are retained and how they are
weighted. \emph{Structural repair} minimizes an internal graph objective:
the weight of edges deleted to restore acyclicity. \emph{Ranking
extraction} turns the (possibly repaired) graph into an ordering.
\emph{Downstream evaluation} scores that ordering against external relevance
judgments with a retrieval metric such as normalized discounted cumulative
gain (nDCG). Repair optimizes agreement with the graph's own edges; nDCG
measures agreement with qrels. Nothing in the repair objective guarantees
that better internal consistency improves retrieval utility
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}).

Existing evidence for a retrieval benefit is inconclusive for three reasons
that prior heuristic-only or structure-only studies cannot close. First,
most evaluations use heuristic repair, so a null retrieval result can be
dismissed as heuristic suboptimality on the graph objective rather than as
evidence about repair itself.
Second, construction is rarely treated as an experimental factor, so repair
effects cannot be separated from upstream choices that create
inconsistency. Third, studies that report structural diagnostics alongside
retrieval metrics rarely test the link under multiplicity-corrected paired
inference; gains on the former are often treated as if they implied gains
on the latter.

This paper is a controlled empirical and methodological audit of that
assumption. It is not a new ranking algorithm, not a new MWFAS formulation,
not a new exact solver, and not a state-of-the-art retrieval model. We treat
construction as an experimental factor and compare unrepaired,
greedy-repaired, and exactly repaired graphs across three vote-construction
regimes on identical candidate sets and stored BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM
scores. Exact MWFAS repair, solved to solver-certified optimality with the
open-source SCIP solver on the stated edge-deletion formulation, is an
\emph{identification control}: if certified optimal repair still yields no
reliable retrieval gain of the detectable scale, greedy suboptimality with
respect to that objective cannot explain the null. Empirically,
construction choices decide whether repairable inconsistency exists at all.
When the candidate pool exceeds the evaluation cutoff, repair changes
top-$k$ membership by an average of $10.6\%$. Yet no
repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG comparison survives Holm-corrected paired
testing in any reported family, and simple graph-free fusion remains
competitive with the best repaired graph method
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

The contribution is threefold and deliberately non-overlapping:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A factorized preference-graph audit.} We separate
    construction, structural intervention, ranking extraction, and
    downstream utility as distinct empirical stages, report them with
    separate evidence, and apply paired, multiplicity-corrected inference
    to repaired-versus-unrepaired comparisons. The reusable pattern is this
    factorization plus an exact-repair diagnostic control
    (Sections~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}, \ref{sec:discussion-practical}).
  \item \textbf{Construction-sensitive structural evidence.} Under the
    studied regimes, construction choices determine whether cyclic
    structure exists; when it does, repair is a real structural
    intervention, including top-$k$ membership change when $P>k$ and
    systematically lower removed weight under exact than under greedy
    repair.
  \item \textbf{Protocol-conditional retrieval evidence.} No
    repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG family survives Holm correction under
    this protocol, while graph-free fusion anchors remain competitive.
    Exact repair narrows the interpretation of that null by ruling out
    greedy suboptimality with respect to the specified MWFAS objective on
    certified instances, not by proving that repair never helps.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope of evidence.}
Confirmatory evidence comes from multi-ranker retrieval on SciDocs, FiQA,
HotpotQA, and BRIGHT with three base scorers and three vote-construction
regimes; inference is query-level within reported Holm families. The
$1{,}025$ certified exact solves establish solver coverage, not $1{,}025$
independent retrieval effects. A six-query LLM pilot is directional only.
Results do not extend to cross-encoder or large-scale LLM rerankers, learned
fusion, or interactive retrieval; they do not prove universal
repaired--unrepaired equivalence; and they do not prove that repair is never
beneficial outside this protocol.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Pairwise Ranking and Preference Aggregation}
\label{sec:related-pairwise}

Pairwise comparisons convert relative judgments into rankings along two
traditions that answer different questions. \emph{Probabilistic preference
modeling} explains noisy outcomes through latent strengths, as in
Bradley--Terry and Plackett--Luce
models~\cite{bradley1952rank,hunter2004mm,plackett1975analysis,luce1959individual},
or recovers a ranking as the stationary distribution of a comparison
Markov chain~\cite{negahban2017rankcentrality}. Combinatorial Hodge theory
separates a globally consistent component from a cyclic residual and
therefore diagnoses inconsistency without treating cycle removal as a
retrieval intervention~\cite{jiang2011hodge,vigna2016spectral}. Learning
methods such as GNNRank instead train digraph embeddings to reduce ranking
upsets on pairwise-comparison graphs~\cite{he2022gnnrank}.
\emph{Combinatorial aggregation and cycle repair}, by contrast, treat the
observed preferences as fixed evidence and seek an ordering or graph edit
that minimizes disagreement, from Kemeny consensus and web-scale
aggregation rules~\cite{kemeny1959mathematics,dwork2001rank} to
approximation algorithms linked to correlation
clustering~\cite{ailon2008aggregating,kenyon2007fewerrors}. Bias-aware
pairwise ranking further shows that the comparison sampling process itself
can introduce systematic bias before any repair step~\cite{ferrara2024biasaware}.

This paper follows the combinatorial tradition operationally---edge-deletion
MWFAS repair---but asks a different empirical question from ranking-recovery
benchmarks that optimize upset or disagreement objectives on comparison
graphs~\cite{he2022gnnrank,vahidi2024mwfas}: whether improving that
graph-internal objective improves retrieval utility against external qrels
(Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}).

\subsection{Pairwise and Graph-Based Ranking in Information Retrieval}
\label{sec:related-ir}

In retrieval, pairwise and listwise comparisons are primarily tools for
producing better rankings, not for auditing repair. Pairwise ranking
prompting and related LLM paradigms ask whether using pairwise (or listwise)
evidence improves effectiveness relative to pointwise
scoring~\cite{qin-etal-2024-large,sun-etal-2023-chatgpt}. Listwise and
setwise systems reduce call cost while reordering
candidates~\cite{pradeep2023rankzephyr,zhuang2024setwise,yoon2024listt5,gangi-reddy-etal-2024-first},
and tournament-style LLM ranking ensembles multiple group outcomes to
mitigate input-order sensitivity~\cite{chen2025tourrank}. Hybrid lexical/dense
pipelines and graph-free fusion (RRF, CombSUM, Borda) remain strong
alternatives that never build a preference
graph~\cite{zhang2023hybrid,cormack2009rrf,fox1994combination,vogt1999fusion}.

A smaller line builds an explicit preference graph and then aggregates it.
\texttt{PRP-Graph} constructs a graph from LLM pairwise prompts and
aggregates for text reranking~\cite{luo2024prpgraph};
\texttt{LLM-RankFusion} mitigates pairwise inconsistency across LLM
rankers~\cite{zeng2026llmrankfusion}. Those systems evaluate whether
graph aggregation of pairwise LLM evidence improves ranking. The present
study holds a score-derived multi-ranker graph fixed and asks whether
\emph{repairing} retained contradictions adds nDCG under corrected
inference---a marginal-repair question rather than a pairwise-vs-pointwise
bake-off~\cite{thakur2021beir,craswell2021overview}.

\subsection{Inconsistency, Cycles, and Preference Repair}
\label{sec:related-repair}

A directed preference graph admits a total order consistent with all
retained edges if and only if it is acyclic; a DAG need not be transitively
closed, and several linear extensions may remain. Aggregating disagreeing
sources routinely creates cycles. Removing a minimum-weight edge set to
restore acyclicity is the NP-hard MWFAS
problem~\cite{karp1972reducibility}, addressed by greedy cycle
heuristics~\cite{eades1993heuristic}, approximation schemes connected to
correlation clustering~\cite{kenyon2007fewerrors,ailon2008aggregating}, and
exact linear-ordering / branch-and-cut
methods~\cite{groetschel1984cutting,baharev2021exact}. Exact FAS
optimization targets a graph objective; this paper uses certified exact
solves only as an identification control for downstream retrieval
interpretation, not as a new formulation or solver. Related combinatorial
MWFAS ranking work likewise optimizes ranking-recovery metrics rather than
retrieval nDCG under construction-factor audits~\cite{vahidi2024mwfas}.

LLM-as-judge settings exhibit the same cycles through positional and order
effects~\cite{zheng2023judging,shi2025judging}. Mitigation methods may
improve agreement or acyclicity and report gains on LLM evaluation,
selection, or RLAIF objectives---for example preference-graph ensemble
denoising~\cite{hu2026acyclic} and recent cycle-breaking preference-graph
rewards for reinforcement learning from AI
feedback~\cite{liu2025votinggraph}. Those results support treating
inconsistency as real, but they do not by themselves establish that
repairing a document-retrieval preference graph improves qrel-based nDCG
under multiplicity-corrected paired tests.

\subsection{Structural Quality Versus Downstream Utility}
\label{sec:related-utility}

Structural diagnostics (cyclicity, removed feedback-arc weight) are often
reported without a corrected test against a downstream task metric. That
pattern mirrors the data-quality distinction between \emph{intrinsic} and
\emph{contextual} quality~\cite{wang1996beyond} and the broader lesson that
upstream choices can reshape conclusions when only the final metric is
inspected~\cite{sambasivan2021datacascades,northcutt2021labelerrors}.
Retrieval comparisons require multiplicity-aware paired
testing~\cite{urbano2019statistical,carterette2012multiple,ihemelandu2024multiple},
and non-significant results are not equivalence
claims~\cite{lakens2018equivalence}. We found limited prior preference-graph
and FAS ranking work that jointly (i)~varies construction as a factor,
(ii)~compares unrepaired, greedy, and certified-exact repair, and
(iii)~tests repaired-versus-unrepaired retrieval utility under
prespecified Holm families on multi-ranker document retrieval. The
factorized audit in Section~\ref{sec:introduction} targets that gap.

\section{Background and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:background}

This section formalizes the pipeline stages from
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. Instantiation details appear in
Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}.

\subsection{Retrieval and Candidate Rankings}
\label{sec:retrieval-setting}

Let $q \in \mathcal{Q}$ denote a query drawn from a fixed query set, and let
$D_q = \{d_1,\dots,d_n\}$ denote its query-specific candidate document set:
the pool of documents that will be compared and ranked for $q$. Candidate
sets are formed upstream of our pipeline, typically from the union
of documents scored by one or more base rankers, and are held fixed.

A base ranker $s \in S$ assigns a score $x_{q,s}(d) \in \mathbb{R}$ to each
document $d \in D_q$ it evaluates. Scores may be missing for some documents.
Because raw scores from different rankers are not directly comparable, they
are normalized per query and per ranker before cross-ranker comparison. The
normalization used in the canonical experiments is per-query, per-ranker
min-max scaling,
\begin{equation}
\bar{x}_{q,s}(d) =
\frac{x_{q,s}(d) - \min_{d' \in D_{q,s}} x_{q,s}(d')}
     {\max_{d' \in D_{q,s}} x_{q,s}(d') - \min_{d' \in D_{q,s}} x_{q,s}(d')},
\label{eq:minmax}
\end{equation}
where $D_{q,s}\subseteq D_q$ is the subset of candidates scored by ranker $s$
for query $q$. If all retained scores for a query--ranker pair are equal
(range below $10^{-12}$), the normalized vector is set to all zeros. Missing
candidates are excluded from the range calculation and remain abstentions
when votes are formed. Pairwise votes are built from normalized scores
$\bar{x}_{q,s}$ (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}). The unnormalized construction
is used only for a robustness ablation, as raw score margins are not a
scale-neutral basis for comparing rankers.

Relevance judgments (qrels), which provide graded or binary labels for
query-document pairs, are drawn from external human annotations and are not
derived from ranker scores or preference graphs. All retrieval metrics are
computed against these external judgments at a fixed cutoff $k$. For
example, nDCG$@k$ scores only the top-$k$ documents in a ranking. The
relationship between the candidate pool size $P = |D_q|$ and the cutoff $k$
is an explicit design choice (Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}). Repair
can change the top-$k$ set only when $P > k$.

\subsection{Derived Weighted Preference Graph}
\label{sec:pref-graph}

For each query $q$, the normalized ranker scores $\bar{x}_{q,s}$ are
converted into a weighted directed preference graph
\begin{equation}
G_q = (V_q, E_q, w_q), \qquad V_q = D_q,
\label{eq:graph-def}
\end{equation}
where a directed edge $(u,v) \in E_q$ with weight $w_q(u,v) > 0$ represents
the strength of evidence for ranking document $u$ ahead of document $v$. The
graph is a derived object, not ground truth. Its structure (edges,
directions, and weights) is determined by an explicit construction
procedure applied to the normalized scores, not by intrinsic properties of
the documents.

Construction proceeds ranker by ranker and pair by pair. For each ranker $s$
and each scored document pair $\{u,v\} \subseteq D_q$, a directional vote
($u \succ v$ or $v \succ u$) is cast based on the normalized scores. The vote
strength is derived from the normalized score margin,
$|\bar{x}_{q,s}(u) - \bar{x}_{q,s}(v)|$. Ties in normalized score or pairs
where a document is unscored result in an abstention. These are treated as
an absence of evidence, not evidence of indifference. Votes are aggregated
per directed pair. A retention rule, the \emph{vote-construction regime},
then determines which pairs are retained as edges in $G_q$.

This study evaluates three vote-construction regimes, varying only the
retention rule while holding the candidate set and ranker scores fixed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{ms2}} (conservative): retain a direction only with
    majority support from rankers and an aggregated margin above a
    threshold. This conservative regime suppresses most two-way
    contradictions and typically produces acyclic graphs.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{ms1}} (permissive): retain every ranker-supported
    directional vote. This permissive regime allows for direct mutual
    contradictions—where both $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$ are retained—and thus
    longer cycles.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}}: similar to \texttt{ms1}, but
    drops pairs with bidirectional support during vote construction. This
    isolates cyclicity arising from longer-cycle nontransitivity rather
    than direct mutual contradictions. It is distinct from a post-hoc
    modification of an \texttt{ms1} graph.
\end{itemize}
These regimes are not proposed as optimal construction rules but serve as a
controlled sweep to establish graph construction as a first-class
experimental factor. Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup} reports its strong
effect on graph cyclicity. Since the vote-construction regime determines
the level of inconsistency, any observed repair effect must be interpreted
in the context of the regime that produced the graph.

\subsection{Cycles and Structural Inconsistency}
\label{sec:cycles}

A weighted directed graph $G_q$ is \emph{acyclic} if it contains no directed
cycle $u_1 \to u_2 \to \dots \to u_m \to u_1$; equivalently, its strongly
connected components are all singletons. An acyclic graph $G_q$ admits at
least one consistent linear extension. Conversely, a cyclic graph cannot be
represented by any single total order over $D_q$ without violating at least
one edge.

We report two specific measures of structural inconsistency rather than a
single omnibus cyclicity score. The first is the cyclic-query rate: the
fraction of queries for which $G_q$ is cyclic under a given construction
regime. The second measure distinguishes cyclicity from direct mutual
contradictions (i.e., pairs retained in both directions) from that which
persists after their removal. We report two related diagnostics:
post-aggregation deletion of reciprocal edges from an \texttt{ms1} graph,
and the separate \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual} construction. These are not
guaranteed to coincide because they handle mutual contradictions at
different stages and may use different thresholds
(Section~\ref{sec:results-structure}). Conflating them would misrepresent
the source of cyclicity.

Acyclicity is a purely structural property. It guarantees the existence of a
consistent total order but does not guarantee the correctness of the
retained edges, agreement with relevance judgments, or superior performance
of any particular ranking extraction rule. We distinguish between
transitivity, acyclicity, evidential correctness, and retrieval optimality.
Accordingly, Sections~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair} and~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}
separate the repair objective (feedback-arc weight) from the evaluation
metric (nDCG).

\subsection{Minimum-Weight Feedback-Arc-Set Repair}
\label{sec:mwfas-repair}

Given a preference graph $G_q = (V_q, E_q, w_q)$, repair seeks a
minimum-weight feedback arc set $F_q^\star \subseteq E_q$ whose removal
renders the graph acyclic:
\begin{equation}
F_q^\star = \arg\min_{F \subseteq E_q} \sum_{e \in F} w_q(e)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad (V_q,\, E_q \setminus F) \text{ is acyclic}.
\label{eq:mwfas}
\end{equation}
This is the minimum-weight feedback arc set (MWFAS) problem
(Section~\ref{sec:related-repair}); the repaired graph is
$\widetilde{G}_q = (V_q, E_q \setminus F_q^\star, w_q)$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas} is purely structural: it minimizes disagreement
with the graph's own edges and does not mention relevance judgments or
nDCG.

We evaluate two co-equal solvers for Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}.
\textbf{Greedy edge-deletion repair} iteratively finds some directed cycle
(NetworkX DFS order) and deletes that cycle's minimum-weight edge (weight
ties broken by that cycle's edge order), repeating until acyclic. It is
deterministic on a fixed graph but not minimum-weight; on cyclic queries
here it typically \emph{over-removes} total weight relative to a certified
optimum (Section~\ref{sec:results-exact}).
\textbf{Exact edge-deletion repair} solves Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas} with
the linear-ordering MIP below
(PySCIPOpt/SCIP~\cite{groetschel1984cutting,baharev2021exact}). Let
$\mathrm{before}[u,v]\in\{0,1\}$ mean $u$ precedes $v$ in a total order of
$V_q$:
\begin{align}
\begin{split}
\min\quad
&\sum_{(u,v)\in E_q} w_q(u,v)\,\mathrm{before}[v,u] \\
\text{s.t.}\quad
&\mathrm{before}[u,v]+\mathrm{before}[v,u]=1
  \quad\forall\, u\neq v, \\
&\mathrm{before}[a,b]+\mathrm{before}[b,c]+\mathrm{before}[c,a]\le 2
  \quad\forall\, \text{distinct }a,b,c, \\
&\mathrm{before}[u,v]\in\{0,1\}
  \quad\forall\, u\neq v.
\end{split}
\label{eq:mwfas-mip}
\end{align}
Edge $(u,v)$ is removed iff $\mathrm{before}[v,u]=1$. Reciprocal pairs may
both contribute and both be removed. The optimum equals
Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}. Graphs enter the exact-repair evidence only with
SCIP proven-optimal status (relative gap $0.0$).

Exact repair is a \emph{diagnostic control} for greedy
\emph{suboptimality w.r.t.\ Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}} on certified
instances: no feasible edge deletion restores acyclicity with less weight
under that objective. Exactness does not extend to retrieval utility,
relevance-aware repair, edge reversal, soft aggregation, or other
interventions. No scalability claim is made beyond the sizes studied here.

\subsection{Ranking Extraction and Evaluation}
\label{sec:extraction-eval}

A final ranking is extracted from the unrepaired graph $G_q$, the repaired
graph $\widetilde{G}_q$, or the upstream ranker signals alone. The canonical
experiments compare four families of extraction rules. Graph-dependent rules
are evaluated on both unrepaired and repaired graphs:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prior-only}: ranks by an upstream per-document score,
    independent of the graph.
  \item \textbf{Graph-free fusion}: methods such as reciprocal rank fusion,
    CombSUM, and Borda-style fusion, which operate on the constituent
    rankers' outputs without constructing a preference graph.
  \item \textbf{Graph-only extraction}: a Copeland-style score
    $s_{\mathrm{cop}}(u) = \deg^+(u) - \deg^-(u)$ (unweighted
    out-degree minus in-degree); a weighted balance score
    $s_{\mathrm{bal}}(u) = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} w(u,v) - \sum_{(v,u) \in E}
    w(v,u)$ (weighted out-edge mass minus in-edge mass); and one or more
    stationary-distribution scores computed from a Markov chain over the
    (weighted) graph, in the spirit of Rank Centrality
    (Section~\ref{sec:related-pairwise}). Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}
    details the specific instantiations of these rules, which include
    multiple Markov-chain variants and other centrality measures. Each rule
    is applied to both the unrepaired ($G_q$) and repaired
    ($\widetilde{G}_q$) graphs, holding the rule constant to isolate the
    effect of repair.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid prior-plus-graph extraction}: the graph score is
    combined with a graph-free prior score,
    \begin{equation}
    h_q(d) = \hat{s}^{\mathrm{prior}}_q(d)
             + \alpha\, \hat{s}^{\mathrm{graph}}_q(d),
    \label{eq:hybrid}
    \end{equation}
    where both score components are independently min-max normalized per
    query. The primary analysis uses $\alpha=0.3$, with other values tested
    for robustness. The prior $\hat{s}^{\mathrm{prior}}_q$ is reciprocal
    rank fusion over the three base rankers on $D_q$ with $k{=}60$, not a
    simple score sum; final ties break by document id. The method is
    unrepaired when $\hat{s}^{\mathrm{graph}}_q$ is computed from $G_q$ and
    repaired when computed from $\widetilde{G}_q$.
\end{itemize}

Retrieval effectiveness is measured with normalized discounted cumulative
gain (nDCG)~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated}: for relevance labels
$\mathrm{rel}_1,\dots,\mathrm{rel}_k$ at ranks $1,\dots,k$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\mathrm{rel}_i}-1}{\log_2(i+1)},
\qquad
\mathrm{nDCG}@k = \frac{\mathrm{DCG}@k}{\mathrm{IDCG}@k},
\label{eq:ndcg}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{IDCG}@k$ is the ideal DCG. We compute nDCG per query and
compare unrepaired and repaired variants of each extraction rule with all
other factors held fixed
(Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}). Because
Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas} uses only graph-internal weights while nDCG uses
external qrels, optimizing repair is not the same problem as improving
nDCG; Sections~\ref{sec:experimental_setup} and~\ref{sec:results} test
whether the two are linked empirically under this protocol.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

This section instantiates Section~\ref{sec:background}: research questions,
pipeline, datasets, rankers, construction, repair, extraction, baselines,
metrics, and statistical protocol for the evidence in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. It documents what was measured and how, not
outcomes.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The study answers four questions that operationalize the separation of
construction, structural repair, and retrieval utility
(Section~\ref{sec:introduction}):

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How much structural inconsistency -- cyclicity and
    contradictory edge mass -- is present in derived multi-ranker
    preference graphs, and how strongly does it depend on graph-construction
    choices (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}) rather than on the underlying
    documents?
  \item[RQ2] How do greedy and exact minimum-weight feedback-arc-set repair
    (Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}) differ in the structural change they
    produce -- removed edge weight, the chosen feedback arc set, and
    top-$k$ membership when the candidate pool exceeds the evaluation
    cutoff -- on the same graphs?
  \item[RQ3] Does preference-graph repair, heuristic or exact, produce a
    statistically reliable improvement in downstream retrieval
    effectiveness (nDCG) relative to the unrepaired graph, once paired
    inference and multiplicity correction are applied -- and how do
    repaired graph methods compare with simple graph-free fusion anchors?
  \item[RQ4] Is any observed retrieval null in RQ3 attributable to
    greedy-repair suboptimality rather than to repair itself, once exact
    MWFAS is used as a diagnostic control under matched construction,
    pooling, and extraction choices?
\end{description}

These questions concern the marginal effect of repair under this protocol,
not a learned repair policy or a retrieval leaderboard. Fairness for
RQ3--RQ4 means holding pool, scores, construction, extraction, and cutoff
fixed while varying only the repair branch
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\subsection{Experimental Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Every reported result is produced by the same nine-stage pipeline, run
once per query under each combination of dataset, vote-construction
regime, and evaluation cell studied:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item obtain stored per-document scores from each base ranker
    (Section~\ref{sec:rankers}) for a fixed candidate pool;
  \item normalize scores per query and per ranker
    (Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax});
  \item derive weighted pairwise votes from normalized score margins
    (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph});
  \item construct the query-specific weighted directed preference graph
    $G_q$ under one vote-construction regime (Section~\ref{sec:construction-protocol});
  \item extract unrepaired rankings directly from $G_q$ and from the prior
    score alone;
  \item repair $G_q$ into $\widetilde{G}_q$ with greedy cycle-peeling or
    exact SCIP-based MWFAS solving (Section~\ref{sec:repair-protocol});
  \item extract repaired rankings from $\widetilde{G}_q$ using the same
    extraction rule applied to $G_q$ (Section~\ref{sec:extraction-methods});
  \item evaluate every extracted ranking against relevance judgments with
    nDCG at a prespecified cutoff (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}); and
  \item conduct paired, multiplicity-corrected statistical inference on
    repaired-versus-unrepaired differences (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}).
\end{enumerate}
Stages 1--4 are shared by every downstream comparison; stages 5--9 are
repeated once per extraction rule and once per repair method (unrepaired,
greedy, exact) being compared. No stage regenerates upstream retrieval:
stage 1 reuses fixed, versioned score files, so every reported statistic
is reproducible from stored intermediates without repeating retrieval or
querying a paid API (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).

\subsection{Datasets and Retrieval Tasks}
\label{sec:datasets}

The study uses four public retrieval benchmarks chosen for domain diversity
under a shared offline reranking setting, not as coverage of all retrieval
workloads: SciDocs~\cite{cohan2020scidocs} (scientific document /
citation-related retrieval), FiQA~\cite{maia2018fiqa} (financial
question--answering), HotpotQA~\cite{yang2018hotpotqa} (multi-hop Wikipedia
QA), and BRIGHT~\cite{su2025bright} (public \texttt{examples} configuration;
reasoning-oriented retrieval). SciDocs, FiQA, and HotpotQA also appear in
BEIR-style zero-shot benchmarking~\cite{thakur2021beir}. Together they
stress construction and repair across scientific, financial, multi-hop, and
reasoning tasks with public qrels; they do not claim generality to web-scale
search, conversational retrieval, or interactive sessions.

Base-ranker scores, query IDs, and relevance judgments are reused from
fixed stored files; no upstream retrieval is regenerated. The evaluation
setting is candidate-pool reranking over previously retrieved documents
rather than a full collection scan. Queries need at least one
qrels-eligible relevant document among candidates scored by at least one
ranker; this excludes 18 of HotpotQA's 70 stored queries and leaves every
other dataset's stored queries usable. Candidate pools are the union of
documents scored by at least one base ranker, truncated by the primary pool
policy (Section~\ref{sec:construction-protocol}); Table~\ref{tab:setup}
reports depths and evaluated $(P,k)$ cells. Usable query counts (50--120 per
dataset) are modest by large-track standards, which is why power and
minimum-detectable-effect reporting accompany the confirmatory nulls
(Section~\ref{sec:statistics}).

\subsection{Base Rankers and Judgment Sources}
\label{sec:rankers}

Three base rankers supply heterogeneous scores that become pairwise votes.
Their role is a controlled multi-source substrate for construction and
repair, not the strongest available retrieval stack.
\textbf{BM25}~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic} (Okapi BM25, $k_1=1.5$,
$b=0.75$) and \textbf{TF-IDF}~\cite{salton1988termweighting} (cosine
logarithmic TF--IDF) are classical lexical signals;
\textbf{MiniLM}~\cite{wang2020minilm} (\texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} cosine
embeddings, not fine-tuned here) is a fixed small dense signal. They are
deliberately transparent and weaker than cross-encoders or large LLM
rerankers so that any repair effect can be isolated from upstream ranker
changes. All three score the same candidate pool; higher scores indicate
stronger estimated relevance before normalization
(Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax}).

\subsection{Preference-Graph Construction}
\label{sec:construction-protocol}

The primary construction protocol normalizes every ranker's scores with
per-query, per-ranker min-max scaling (Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax}) and
extracts directional votes from normalized score margins, following
Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}. Vote retention uses a retention-matched
thresholding rule that never consults qrels or nDCG. On each dataset, a
raw-margin (unnormalized) construction with the same support rule supplies
targets: per-ranker retained-vote rates and the regime's retained directed
edge count. For the normalized protocol, each ranker's vote-margin
threshold is the empirical quantile of that ranker's pooled pairwise
normalized margins matching the raw retained-vote rate
($q=1-\mathrm{target\_rate}$). Given those vote thresholds, the
regime-level aggregate weight threshold is chosen by a discrete search over
candidate aggregate weights to minimize the absolute gap in retained edge
count versus the raw baseline for that regime (ties break toward the smaller
threshold). Vote-margin thresholds are therefore per dataset and per
ranker, shared across regimes within a dataset; aggregate thresholds are
per dataset and per regime; neither is per query. Thresholds are fixed from
the full usable query set of that dataset before retrieval metrics are
computed. This is unsupervised collection-level protocol calibration: it
uses only stored ranker scores, never qrels or nDCG, so it is not
relevance-label leakage, but it is transductive with respect to the
evaluated query list (no held-out calibration split;
Section~\ref{sec:limitations-internal}). Matching is approximate
(minimum absolute edge-count gap), not exact equality of every rate.
Canonical values appear in Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}. Support requirements
are fixed per regime:
\texttt{ms2} requires support from at least two of the three rankers;
\texttt{ms1} and \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual} require support from at least
one ranker, differing in whether contested (two-way) pairs are dropped at
vote construction (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}).

Candidate pools use one primary policy: for each query, the top-$P$
documents of a reciprocal-rank-fusion ranking computed over the union of
the three rankers' native (unnormalized) score lists. This ``RRF-centered''
pool policy is one specific, named choice among several equally valid
ways to truncate a candidate pool, and it determines which documents are
eligible to appear in $D_q$ at all -- a decision made once, upstream of
score normalization and graph construction. Four alternative,
independently defined pool policies (equal per-ranker depth, round-robin
interleaving across rankers, a single-ranker pool, and a CombSUM-ranked
pool) are evaluated as a robustness check (Section~\ref{sec:results}) to
test whether the study's structural and retrieval conclusions depend on
this specific choice.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:construction} summarizes graph construction for a single
query, reflecting the procedure implemented in the repository's graph- and
vote-construction code. The description is intentionally compact: the
contribution is not a new preference extractor, but a controlled study of
how these construction choices affect cyclicity, repair, and downstream
retrieval.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Preference-graph construction for one query}
\label{alg:construction}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Query $q$; candidate set $D_q$; base rankers $S=\{\mathrm{BM25},
  \mathrm{TF\text{-}IDF}, \mathrm{MiniLM}\}$ with scores $x_{q,s}$;
  construction regime $r \in \{\texttt{ms2}, \texttt{ms1},
  \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}\}$
\Ensure Weighted directed preference graph $G_q=(V_q,E_q,w_q)$
\State $V_q \gets D_q$; initialize an empty weighted edge accumulator
\For{each ranker $s \in S$}
  \State Normalize $x_{q,s}$ to $\bar{x}_{q,s}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax})
  \For{each unordered candidate pair $\{u,v\} \subseteq D_q$ scored by $s$}
    \If{$|\bar{x}_{q,s}(u) - \bar{x}_{q,s}(v)|$ is below the vote-margin
      threshold, or $s$ does not score both $u$ and $v$}
      \State Abstain on $\{u,v\}$ for ranker $s$
    \Else
      \State Accumulate directional support for $u \succ v$ or $v \succ u$
        (whichever $\bar{x}_{q,s}$ supports), weighted by the normalized
        margin
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Apply regime $r$'s support rule and aggregate weight threshold to
  every accumulated directed pair
\If{$r = \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}$}
  \State Remove every unordered pair retained in both directions
\EndIf
\State $E_q \gets$ every retained directed pair with positive aggregated weight
\State \Return $G_q = (V_q, E_q, w_q)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Repair Methods}
\label{sec:repair-protocol}

Two repair methods solve Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}, reported as co-equal
throughout; their roles are defined in Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}.
\textbf{Greedy repair} is the repository's deterministic cycle-peeling
edge-deletion heuristic described in Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair} (not
the classical Eades--Lin--Smyth linear-arrangement
heuristic~\cite{eades1993heuristic}). It deletes edges until the graph is
acyclic; it does not claim a minimum-weight feedback arc set.
\textbf{Exact repair} solves Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas-mip} with open-source
SCIP via PySCIPOpt (pinned version 6.2.1), requiring proven-optimal status
(relative gap $0.0$) and a 300-second per-query safety time limit that was
never approached at the sizes studied here. The formulation was
cross-checked against brute-force enumeration on synthetic and real query
graphs. Exact repair remains the diagnostic control of
Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair} (RQ4; Section~\ref{sec:results-exact}).

\subsection{Ranking Extraction Methods}
\label{sec:extraction-methods}

Extraction methods fall into four groups, extending
Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}'s general definitions with the specific
methods this study evaluates and stating precisely which methods enter
which comparison family (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\textbf{Prior-only and graph-free fusion} rank candidates without
constructing or consulting a preference graph: the prior-only method ranks
by the same RRF prior used in Equation~\eqref{eq:hybrid}; reciprocal rank
fusion, CombSUM, and Borda-style fusion combine the three rankers'
individual per-query rankings or scores
directly~\cite{cormack2009rrf,fox1994combination}.

\textbf{Graph-only extraction} computes a score from $G_q$ or
$\widetilde{G}_q$ alone. Table~\ref{tab:extraction-params} records the
implementation parameters used throughout. The Copeland score
$s_{\mathrm{cop}}$ and balance score $s_{\mathrm{bal}}$
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}) are evaluated on every dataset, regime,
and repair method. Two related but distinct Markov-chain scores are also
evaluated: a damped stationary-distribution score with uniform restart
(``Markov''), and an undamped Rank Centrality chain with explicit
self-loops~\cite{negahban2017rankcentrality}. Standard graph PageRank on
the reversed preference graph~\cite{page1999pagerank}, Bradley--Terry MM
MLE with edge weights as win
counts~\cite{bradley1952rank,hunter2004mm}, and (on repaired DAGs only)
NetworkX topological order plus prior-priority topological order complete
the set.

\textbf{Hybrid prior-plus-graph extraction} combines a graph-only score
with the RRF prior via Equation~\eqref{eq:hybrid}. Copeland and balance
hybrids use $\alpha=0.3$ in the primary analysis, with
$\alpha \in \{0.1,0.3,0.5,1.0\}$ as a robustness check; a Markov hybrid at
the same fixed $\alpha$ enters the expanded family
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The primary retrieval metric is normalized discounted cumulative gain
(nDCG) at a prespecified cutoff $k$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:ndcg}), evaluated
per query and averaged across a dataset's usable queries; higher values
indicate better agreement with graded relevance judgments. Mean
reciprocal rank (MRR) and mean average
precision (MAP) are computed as secondary diagnostics only and are never
used as a primary confirmatory metric. All three are computed at the same
evaluated $(P,k)$ cell (Table~\ref{tab:setup}) for every method and repair
variant being compared.

Following Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}, the repair objective,
structural diagnostics, and retrieval metrics are reported separately and
never treated as interchangeable proxies.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:statistics}

The unit of statistical inference is the query: repaired-minus-unrepaired
nDCG once per query. Paired evaluation is required because methods share
queries and pools~\cite{urbano2019statistical}. Primary greedy confirmatory
families (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval-holm}) use a two-sided Monte Carlo
paired sign-flip test on the mean difference ($10{,}000$ sign patterns,
seed $17$, add-one $p$), matching the canonical
\texttt{full\_calibrated\_core} / pool-cutoff pipelines; zeros are
flip-invariant. Holm step-down~\cite{holm1979simple} at $\alpha=0.05$
controls family-wise error within each prespecified family
(Table~\ref{tab:holm-families})~\cite{carterette2012multiple,ihemelandu2024multiple},
but does not model hierarchical dependence when the same queries recur
across regimes, pools, or extractors.

Exact-repair, power/MDE, and related analyses use
\texttt{statistical\_inference}: the same two-sided sign-flip null, with
exact enumeration when nonzero differences $m\le 20$ else Monte Carlo
($10{,}000$, seed $17$), plus percentile/basic/BCa bootstrap $95\%$
intervals ($10{,}000$ resamples, seed $13$)~\cite{efron1993introduction}.
Intervals, leave-one-out influence, BH FDR alongside
Holm~\cite{benjamini1995controlling}, and $80\%$-power MDE/TOST at
prespecified margins~\cite{lakens2018equivalence} characterize detectable
scale and limited equivalence; they do not replace confirmatory Holm or
convert nonsignificance into blanket equivalence
(Section~\ref{sec:related-utility}).

$63.5\%$ (HotpotQA) to $99.2\%$ (SciDocs). Most of that cyclicity is
direct, two-way contradiction rather than longer-range nontransitivity:
the construction-time mutual-drop regime (\texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual})
reduces the cyclic-query rate to between $1.9\%$ (HotpotQA) and $30.8\%$

reciprocal edges from the already-aggregated \texttt{ms1} digraph, whereas

BRIGHT, but differ on SciDocs ($10.8\%$ post-mutual \texttt{ms1} versus

distinct residual longer-cycle structure

close to a single-ranker graph with multi-ranker labels attached, and
normalization is what restores a genuinely mixed graph.

\subsection{Structural Effect of Repair}
\label{sec:results-repair-structure}

\paragraph{Question (RQ2).}
Where graphs are cyclic, how much weight does repair remove, and does it
change top-$k$ membership when $P>k$?

Repair is structurally active precisely where Section~\ref{sec:results-structure}
shows a graph has contradictory structure to remove, and inert elsewhere.
Under \texttt{ms2}, repair removes nothing on any query, because no graph
is cyclic. Under \texttt{ms1}, the active regime, repair removes a
mean of $7.4\%$ (SciDocs) to $8.0\%$ (FiQA) of total graph weight; under
\texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}, the fraction still to remove after mutual
pairs are already gone drops to $0.2\%$--$0.4\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:structural-outcomes}).
This confirms that graph construction, not the repair algorithm, is what
decides whether repair has any opportunity to act at all -- a point kept
separate here from whether that activity translates into a retrieval
effect, which Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval} addresses directly.

Repair also changes which documents are visible for evaluation once the
candidate pool exceeds the metric cutoff. At $P=k$, top-$k$ set membership
cannot change by construction: the evaluated prefix is the entire pool, so
repairing the graph can reorder documents but never add or remove one from
the top-$k$ set, and the measured membership-change rate is exactly
$0\%$. Once $P>k$, pooling across all three vote-construction regimes and
every larger-pool evaluation cell (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), the mean
top-$k$ membership-change rate rises to $10.6\%$. Repair is therefore not
merely reordering an already-fixed prefix under $P>k$: it can and does
change which documents are evaluated at all. Whether that structural
activity is accompanied by a reliable retrieval-effectiveness gain is
addressed next.

\subsection{Exact Versus Heuristic Repair}
\label{sec:results-exact}

\paragraph{Question (RQ4).}
Is the RQ3 retrieval null explained by greedy suboptimality relative to a
certified MWFAS edge-deletion optimum?

The comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval} use greedy repair,
which does not generally reach the optimum of the feedback-arc-set
objective (Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}). This subsection reports what
changes when repair is instead solved to certified optimality.

Of the $1{,}026$ nominal query--regime combinations
($342$ usable queries $\times$ three vote-construction regimes), one
BRIGHT query under \texttt{ms2} (\texttt{biology:0}) produces an empty
edge set -- no directed pair meets \texttt{ms2}'s min-support requirement
of two rankers -- and is excluded from the exact-repair study, leaving
$1{,}025$ valid exact-repair instances. Every one of those solves reached
proven optimality: $1{,}025/1{,}025$ query graphs, relative optimality gap
$0.0$, with a mean solve time of $7.4$ms (median $0.25$ms, maximum
$236$ms) against a $300$-second per-query safety limit that was never
approached (Section~\ref{sec:repair-protocol}). Restricted to the $379$
queries (pooled across all three vote-construction regimes) whose raw
graph is actually cyclic, exact repair finds a different feedback arc set
than greedy in $87.9\%$ of them, and removes systematically less total
edge weight: Table~\ref{tab:exact-vs-greedy} and Figure~\ref{fig:exact-vs-greedy}
report the per-dataset gap. Greedy cycle-peeling is therefore a real,
non-trivial overestimate of the minimum weighted feedback arc set on this
evidence, not merely a theoretical possibility.

\subsection{Supporting LLM Evidence}
\label{sec:results-llm}

\paragraph{Question.}
Do real LLM pairwise judgments, on a tiny query sample, contradict the
score-derived RQ3 pattern?

A bounded pilot supplements the score-derived evidence above with real
large-language-model pairwise judgments: six queries, four providers, and
four preference-graph construction variants, analyzed at the query-cluster
level ($n=6$ independent clusters), since the pilot's replicated rows
within a query are repeated measurements, not additional independent
samples. Whole-graph repair's effect on retrieval, estimated the same way
as the score-derived comparisons above, is a mean nDCG change of
$0.00019$ with a cluster-level $95\%$ interval of $[-0.00072, 0.00140]$
(exact sign-flip $p=0.875$): indistinguishable from zero, directionally
consistent with the null finding in Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval}.
Six clusters give this pilot very limited power, so this result is
reported as directional and supporting only -- it does not, on its own,
extend any conclusion in this paper beyond the four benchmarks and
construction regimes making up the main evidence package
(Section~\ref{sec:datasets}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Main Findings}
\label{sec:discussion-main}

Under the canonical protocol, preference-graph repair is a real structural
intervention -- including under exact optimization -- but not a validated
general means of improving retrieval effectiveness
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-structure}--\ref{sec:results-exact}). Structural
consistency and downstream utility behave as distinct objectives here, not
as the same quantity at different resolutions.

That claim is narrow. It does not say repair never helps, that acyclicity
is useless, or that the null of no effect is proven. A corrected null
argues against a general, reliably detectable effect at the scale this
study is powered to observe (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}); smaller
or dataset-specific effects remain possible. On this evidence, treating
acyclicity as a surrogate for retrieval effectiveness is unsupported.

\subsection{Why Structural Improvement Need Not Improve Retrieval}
\label{sec:discussion-mechanism}

Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval} states the formal gap: repair and nDCG
use different information. Several mechanisms are consistent with genuine
structural repair and a null retrieval result, without any one being
isolated experimentally. Feedback-arc-set repair targets least-weight
cycle-breaking edges (Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}), which need not
separate highly relevant from marginally relevant documents near the top of
a ranking. A hybrid prior (Equation~\eqref{eq:hybrid}) can further compress
graph-side influence, consistent with graph-only and hybrid methods not
diverging sharply in Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval}. Different
extraction rules map the same repaired graph differently
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction-methods}), contributing to the mixed-sign
point estimates in Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap-forest}. And pairwise weights
and qrels are built from different evidence
(Sections~\ref{sec:pref-graph}, \ref{sec:retrieval-setting}), so internal
consistency and relevance agreement can vary independently. These accounts
are consistent with the design and observed pattern, not causal claims
established by a dedicated test.

\subsection{Exact Repair as a Diagnostic Control}
\label{sec:discussion-exact}

A heuristic-only study leaves open graph-objective suboptimality as an
alternative explanation. Section~\ref{sec:results-exact} addresses that
alternative for this evidence only with respect to the specified
edge-deletion MWFAS objective on solver-certified instances: exact repair
removes less edge weight than greedy (typical over-removal by the
heuristic) yet yields no Holm-surviving retrieval gain against the
unrepaired graph under the evaluated constructions, extraction rules,
datasets, and detectable effect scale. Exact SCIP repair is therefore a
methodological identification control (Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}), not
a novel solver, not a proof that no graph intervention can help retrieval,
and not a recommendation to run exact MWFAS at production scale.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Literature}
\label{sec:discussion-literature}

The finding that repair is structurally active but not reliably
retrieval-improving sits beside the closest lines in
Table~\ref{tab:closest-works}. Pairwise LLM ranking and graph aggregation
ask whether pairwise evidence or inconsistency mitigation improves ranking
relative to alternatives~\cite{qin-etal-2024-large,luo2024prpgraph,zeng2026llmrankfusion};
acyclic preference methods report gains on LLM evaluation, selection, or
RLAIF objectives~\cite{hu2026acyclic,liu2025votinggraph}. Those results are
compatible with a null repair effect for document-retrieval nDCG under the
present construction-factor protocol. The earlier preprint comparison remains
in Table~\ref{tab:preprint-comparison}.

\subsection{Practical Implications}
\label{sec:discussion-practical}

The reusable methodological lesson is the factorized audit itself: treat
construction, structural intervention, extraction, and utility as separate
evidence streams, and use exact MWFAS on a bounded subset as an
identification control when a heuristic null must be stress-tested.
Practitioners should take three points from this evidence: structural
metrics diagnose construction quality but are not validated nDCG
surrogates here; a repair stage is worth evaluating---with unrepaired and
graph-free fusion controls---when a pipeline already builds a preference
graph and treats acyclicity as a quality signal; and exact repair is a
diagnostic tool for graph-objective suboptimality on a representative
subset, not a full-scale deployment recommendation. Report construction,
extraction, and cutoff with any repair claim, and keep structural and
utility metrics as separate streams (Section~\ref{sec:results-structure}).
These are audit practices, not a validated deployment rule or a production
cost claim. Predicting per-query whether repair will help remains future
work.

\subsection{Implications for LLM-Based Ranking}
\label{sec:discussion-llm}

LLM pairwise judgments can induce cycles for the same structural reasons as
score-derived votes (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}). The structural-versus-utility
distinction therefore applies in principle, but this paper's LLM evidence is
only the six-query pilot in Section~\ref{sec:results-llm} and does not
support broad claims about modern LLM rerankers. Extending the controlled
comparison remains future work.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Internal Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-internal}

The pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}) makes sequential design choices --
normalization, vote thresholds, pooling, and hybrid weight $\alpha$ -- that
could interact with the repair effect. Two controls mitigate this:
retention-matched thresholding (Section~\ref{sec:construction-protocol})
ties normalized permissiveness to the raw-margin baseline without using
qrels, and every retrieval comparison holds construction, pooling, and
extraction fixed while varying only repair
(Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval}). Threshold calibration on the same
usable query list remains a transductive protocol choice: a held-out
calibration split was not used and is a natural future robustness check.
What also remains open is $\alpha$-dependence beyond the reported sweep
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction-methods}). Paired sign-flip with Holm
correction (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}) assumes exchangeable paired
differences within a family; query-level pairing supports that assumption
within a cell, but the same queries recur across regimes and pool sizes.
Holm therefore controls cell-wise family-wise error among the listed tests
without a hierarchical model of cross-cell dependence; multilevel modeling
of that structure is future work, not a claim of the present analysis.

\subsection{Construct Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-construct}

Acyclicity is one narrow notion of structural consistency
(Section~\ref{sec:cycles}); the reported diagnostics (cyclic-query rate,
removed feedback-arc weight, top-$k$ membership change) measure
graph-internal coherence, not evidential correctness. nDCG is the primary
utility construct; MRR and MAP are secondary diagnostics only
(Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). Pairwise vote weight encodes relative-order
confidence, not absolute relevance
(Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}); the two are related through shared documents
but are not the same measurement
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}).

\subsection{External Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-external}

The main evidence is offline multi-ranker reranking on four public
benchmarks with score-derived graphs
(Sections~\ref{sec:datasets},~\ref{sec:rankers}), not web-scale or
conversational retrieval. Pools are shallow (35--50) and RRF-centered;
base rankers are BM25/TF-IDF/MiniLM as controlled score sources, not a
leaderboard. The real-LLM pilot is the six-query addendum of
Section~\ref{sec:results-llm}. Cross-encoders, stronger dense/hybrid
stacks, listwise/tournament LLM
rankers~\cite{chen2025tourrank,pradeep2023rankzephyr}, and neural graph
rankers are incomparable as unrepaired-versus-repaired cells and remain
future work. Transfer may also fail under relevance-aware weights,
edge-reversal or soft repair, sparse comparisons, deeper pools,
interactive retrieval, or effects below the corrected detectable scale.

\subsection{Statistical Conclusion Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-statistical}

Confirmatory claims use Holm-corrected paired inference over reported
families (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}). No Holm rejection is not proof of
no effect. The study can rule out a general effect at the scale of the
observed point estimates relative to the corrected MDE
(Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}), but smaller effects remain
possible. TOST supports narrow equivalence only for cells that meet the
prespecified margin; equivalence in a minority of cells does not establish
blanket practical equivalence. BH is reported as a less conservative
robustness comparison, not a replacement for confirmatory Holm. The
real-LLM pilot uses cluster-level ($n=6$) inference
(Section~\ref{sec:statistics}) and is not confirmatory.

\subsection{Computational Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations-computational}

Exact MWFAS reaches certified optimality quickly at the query-graph sizes
studied here (Section~\ref{sec:results-exact}); larger graphs and deeper
candidate pools are not evaluated, so scalability conclusions stop at the
reported solve-time distribution. Exact repair remains the diagnostic
control of Section~\ref{sec:discussion-exact}. Greedy cycle-peeling remains
the practical choice for larger graphs because exact MWFAS is NP-hard in
general (Section~\ref{sec:related-repair}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study establishes, under the reported protocol, that construction
choices decide whether preference graphs are cyclic; that greedy and exact
repair both restore acyclicity where structure exists, with exact repair
removing less weight; and that neither yields a Holm-surviving
repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG gain in the canonical, larger-pool, or
exact-repair families -- even when larger-pool repair changes top-$k$
membership. Exact repair rules out greedy suboptimality with respect to the
specified edge-deletion MWFAS objective on solver-certified instances under
the evaluated constructions, extraction rules, datasets, and detectable
effect scale, without revealing a hidden retrieval gain of that scale.
Equivalence evidence remains limited to a minority of TOST cells; smaller
effects below the corrected minimum-detectable scale remain possible.

The study does not establish that repair never helps, that repaired and
unrepaired rankings are universally equivalent, or that exact MWFAS should
be run at production scale. Its methodological usefulness is the factorized
audit: report construction, structural diagnostics, extraction, and
downstream metrics separately; keep an unrepaired baseline; and use exact
repair as a bounded identification control when a weak downstream result
must be stress-tested (Section~\ref{sec:discussion-practical}). Future
preference-graph and LLM-judge studies should adopt that separation rather
than treating acyclicity as a proxy for task utility.

\section{Data Availability and Reproducibility}

sources: SciDocs, FiQA, HotpotQA, and BRIGHT are cited in

intermediates, figure data, and scripts required to reproduce the reported

usage summaries only. Raw provider request and response payloads are not
