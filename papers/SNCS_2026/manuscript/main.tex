\title[Structural Consistency Is Not Retrieval Utility]{Structural
  Consistency Is Not Retrieval Utility: An Exact-and-Heuristic Audit of
  Preference-Graph Repair for Multi-Ranker Retrieval}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Soroush} \sur{Vahidi}}\email{sv96@njit.edu}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Ying Wu College of Computing},
  \orgname{New Jersey Institute of Technology},
  \orgaddress{\city{Newark}, \state{NJ}, \country{USA}}}

\abstract{\textbf{Purpose:} Preference-graph repair is often assumed to
improve ranking quality by restoring acyclicity. This study investigates
whether, for score-derived multi-ranker retrieval graphs, heuristic or
exact minimum-weight feedback-arc-set repair yields a multiplicity-corrected
retrieval gain, or if structural consistency and retrieval utility must be
treated as separate claims.

\textbf{Methods:} On four public benchmarks, we built query-level preference
graphs from stored BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM scores under three
vote-construction regimes. We compared rankings from unrepaired,
greedy-repaired, and exactly repaired graphs using paired sign-flip tests
and Holm correction. Exact solutions served as a diagnostic control on
heuristic under-repair, not as a production solver.

\textbf{Results:} Construction choices dominated graph structure: a
conservative vote-construction regime was acyclic throughout, while a
permissive regime was cyclic for most queries. Normalizing scores reduced
BM25's conditional edge-weight share from $0.988$ to $0.512$. Repair
removed non-trivial contradictory weight and, when the candidate pool
exceeded the evaluation cutoff, changed top-$k$ membership by an average of
$10.6\%$. However, no repaired-versus-unrepaired normalized discounted
cumulative gain (nDCG) comparison survived Holm correction in the canonical,
larger-pool, or exact-repair families. Graph-free fusion was competitive
(CombSUM $0.554$ versus $0.546$ for the best repaired hybrid). Exact repair
removed less weight than greedy repair yet still yielded no corrected
retrieval gain.

\textbf{Conclusion:} Under this protocol, preference-graph repair is a
structural intervention but not a validated nDCG optimization. Improving the
repair objective—even to certified optimality—does not substitute for
downstream evaluation. Acyclicity metrics should be treated as structural
diagnostics, not as proxies for retrieval utility.}
\keywords{preference graphs, feedback arc set, graph repair, rank aggregation,
  information retrieval, retrieval evaluation}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Pairwise preferences are a natural and increasingly common form of ranking
evidence. Rather than committing to a single scalar relevance score, a
pairwise judgment states only that one document should be ranked ahead of
another for a given query. This evidence arises from multiple ranking
signals (e.g., lexical and dense retrievers, cross-encoders, or large
language models) or directly from systems that elicit pairwise comparisons.
Aggregating these judgments produces a query-specific weighted directed
preference graph, where an edge $u \rightarrow v$ with a corresponding
weight represents the strength of evidence for ranking document $u$ ahead of
document $v$.

This representation is attractive because it preserves rather than collapses
disagreement. However, it is structurally fragile. Because the graph
aggregates partially disagreeing sources, it can contain directed cycles
($u \rightarrow v \rightarrow w \rightarrow u$), meaning no single total
order is consistent with all retained edges. Such cycles are not
pathological edge cases but the expected outcome of aggregating evidence
from imperfectly correlated sources. Since a cycle represents
self-contradictory evidence, acyclicity is a natural objective.
Consequently, cycle repair—removing or reversing a minimum-weight set of
conflicting edges—is an attractive preprocessing step before extracting a
ranking. This intuition is plausible: a self-consistent graph seems a
better input for any subsequent ranking rule.

This plausibility, however, conflates four distinct stages of the
preference-graph pipeline. \emph{Construction} determines which pairwise
votes are retained and how they are weighted, a process sensitive to
upstream choices in score normalization, vote semantics, and thresholding.
\emph{Structural repair} optimizes a purely internal graph objective:
minimizing the total weight of edges removed or reversed to restore
acyclicity. \emph{Ranking extraction} derives a final ordering from the
(possibly repaired) graph using a scoring rule. \emph{Downstream
evaluation} compares this ordering against external relevance judgments
using a retrieval metric unrelated to the repair objective. Repair minimizes
disagreement with the graph's own edges; retrieval metrics score agreement
with an external notion of relevance. The formulation of the repair
objective does not guarantee that minimizing internal disagreement improves
external retrieval effectiveness, a gap often unaddressed in standard
presentations of repair work.

This study addresses the open question of whether improving a preference
graph's structural consistency also improves the retrieval effectiveness of
its derived ranking. Existing evidence is inconclusive for three compounding
reasons. First, most empirical evaluations use heuristic repair methods. Any
observed retrieval effect—positive or null—is thus confounded by the
heuristic's potential suboptimality; a null result might be dismissed as
insufficient repair. Second, graph construction is rarely varied, making it
difficult to isolate the repair step's effect from that of upstream choices
that determine the graph's initial inconsistency. Third, studies reporting
both structural diagnostics and retrieval metrics rarely test for a
statistically significant correlation; improvements in the former are often
implicitly assumed to cause improvements in the latter.

This paper addresses this gap through an empirical and methodological study,
rather than by proposing a new ranking model or optimization algorithm. We
make graph construction an explicit experimental factor, comparing
unrepaired, greedy-repaired, and exactly repaired preference graphs across
three vote-construction regimes using identical candidate sets and stored
scores from BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM. Exact minimum-weight feedback-arc-set
(MWFAS) repair, solved to certified optimality, serves as a diagnostic
control. If optimal repair fails to produce a reliable retrieval gain, then
the suboptimality of a greedy heuristic cannot explain a null result of
similar magnitude. The central empirical finding is not merely that repair
acts only on cyclic graphs. Construction choices determine whether
repairable inconsistency exists. When the candidate pool exceeds the
evaluation cutoff, repair changes top-$k$ membership by an average of
$10.6\%$. Yet, no repaired-versus-unrepaired normalized discounted
cumulative gain (nDCG) comparison survives Holm-corrected paired testing in
any comparison family. Simple graph-free fusion remains competitive with the
best repaired graph method, reinforcing that structural consistency and
retrieval utility are separate claims (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

The contribution is therefore threefold:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An empirical separation of structural and retrieval effects:
    graph-construction choices determine the existence of repairable
    contradictory structure, and repair can alter top-$k$ document
    membership without producing a statistically significant nDCG gain.
  \item A diagnostic control using exact repair: comparing unrepaired graphs
    with those repaired by greedy and optimal MWFAS methods rules out
    heuristic suboptimality as an explanation for the absence of a
    detectable retrieval gain.
  \item A statistically corrected null result: no family of
    repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG comparisons survives Holm correction.
    Simple graph-free fusion methods remain competitive, indicating that
    structural consistency is not a reliable surrogate for retrieval
    utility under this protocol.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope of evidence.}
The evidence is derived from multi-ranker retrieval experiments on four
public benchmarks (SciDocs, FiQA, HotpotQA, and BRIGHT) using three base
scorers and three vote-construction regimes. Confirmatory inference is
performed at the query level within reported Holm families. The $1{,}025$
certified exact solves establish solver coverage, not $1{,}025$ independent
retrieval effects. A six-query pilot study using a large language model is
directional only and not confirmatory. The results do not generalize to
cross-encoder or large-scale LLM rerankers, learned fusion, or interactive
retrieval, nor do they prove that repair is never beneficial.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Pairwise Ranking and Preference Aggregation}
\label{sec:related-pairwise}

Representing evidence as pairwise comparisons rather than absolute scores is
classical in ranking theory. Two main traditions convert pairwise
comparisons ($u \succ v$) into a ranking. The first is \emph{probabilistic
preference modeling}: Bradley--Terry-type models explain observed comparison
outcomes through latent per-item strengths estimated by maximum
likelihood~\cite{bradley1952rank,hunter2004mm}, and Plackett--Luce models
generalize this idea to full or partial
orderings~\cite{plackett1975analysis,luce1959individual}. Rank Centrality
recovers a ranking as the stationary distribution of a Markov chain built
from pairwise comparisons without fitting a parametric strength
model~\cite{negahban2017rankcentrality}. Combinatorial Hodge theory
decomposes a comparison graph into a globally consistent (gradient)
component and a cyclic (curl/harmonic) residual, giving a spectral account
of the data's distance from a single ranking~\cite{jiang2011hodge,vigna2016spectral}.
Bias-aware pairwise ranking further shows that the sampling process for
comparisons can itself introduce systematic bias, independent of any repair
step~\cite{ferrara2024biasaware}.

The second tradition, \emph{combinatorial rank aggregation and cycle
repair}, seeks an ordering or a modification of the input preferences that
minimizes a disagreement objective. Kemeny's optimal-consensus ranking
minimizes total pairwise disagreement with a set of input
rankings~\cite{kemeny1959mathematics}. Web-scale rank aggregation work
popularized and analyzed Markov-chain, Copeland-style (wins-minus-losses),
and Borda-style (positional) aggregation
rules~\cite{dwork2001rank}. Follow-up work provided approximation
algorithms and complexity results for Kemeny-optimal and related
aggregation problems, connecting them to correlation
clustering~\cite{ailon2008aggregating,kenyon2007fewerrors}. Rank comparison
metrics that tolerate ties and partial overlap, such as the Fagin--Kumar
family, are a companion line of work for measuring the difference between
rankings, such as before and after a repair
step~\cite{fagin2003comparing,fagin2004aggregating}.

These two traditions answer different questions. Probabilistic models
estimate a ranking that best explains noisy comparison outcomes, treating
residual disagreement as statistical noise. Combinatorial repair, in
contrast, treats the observed graph as fixed evidence and seeks to modify
it at minimum cost to enable a single consistent ordering. This paper is
positioned in the second tradition. It applies combinatorial minimum-weight
feedback-arc-set repair (Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}) to a constructed
preference graph. The research question concerns whether optimizing the
combinatorial repair objective also improves retrieval effectiveness, an
external objective it was not designed to optimize.

\subsection{Pairwise and Graph-Based Ranking in Information Retrieval}
\label{sec:related-ir}

Pairwise comparison has become a common building block of modern retrieval
and reranking pipelines. Pairwise ranking prompting elicits document-level
comparisons directly from a large language model and aggregates them into a
ranking~\cite{qin-etal-2024-large}, and pointwise, pairwise, and listwise
LLM reranking paradigms have been compared directly for effectiveness and
cost~\cite{sun-etal-2023-chatgpt}. Listwise and setwise rerankers reduce the
number of language-model calls needed to reorder a candidate
list~\cite{pradeep2023rankzephyr,zhuang2024setwise,gangi-reddy-etal-2024-first,yoon2024listt5}.
Hybrid rerankers combine lexical, dense, and cross-encoder signals within
a single scoring or fusion
step~\cite{zhang2023hybrid,lu2024hyrr,wang2024dapr,nogueira2019passage}.
Graph-free score and rank fusion -- reciprocal rank fusion, CombSUM, and
Borda-style combination -- remains a strong and widely used alternative to
any graph-based aggregation step~\cite{cormack2009rrf,fox1994combination,lee1997analyses,vogt1999fusion}.
Such methods also avoid comparing incompatible raw score magnitudes, a
long-standing concern in fusion and score-normalization
work~\cite{manmatha2002formal,bruch2023analysis,benham2017risk}.

A smaller body of work builds an explicit preference graph from pairwise
document judgments before extracting a ranking, rather than fusing scores or
ranks directly. \texttt{PRP-Graph} builds a ranking graph from pairwise LLM
prompting and aggregates it for text reranking~\cite{luo2024prpgraph};
\texttt{LLM-RankFusion} aggregates several LLM rankers by
explicitly modeling and mitigating pairwise inconsistency in the resulting
comparisons~\cite{zeng2026llmrankfusion}, and confidence-aware or
bias-aware pairwise aggregation methods weight or correct individual
comparisons before they are combined~\cite{ferrara2024biasaware}. This is
the setting the present study investigates: a query-specific
weighted directed graph is built from multiple ranking signals, and a
ranking is extracted either before or after a structural repair step. Our
methodology follows standard retrieval benchmarking practice, such as
reusing stored candidate pools and evaluating with rank-sensitive metrics
like nDCG, consistent with conventions in zero-shot retrieval
benchmarking~\cite{thakur2021beir,craswell2021overview}.

\subsection{Inconsistency, Cycles, and Preference Repair}
\label{sec:related-repair}

A directed preference graph admits at least one total order consistent with
all retained edges if and only if it is acyclic. Acyclicity does not imply
that the observed edge relation is transitively closed: a DAG need not equal
its transitive closure, and several linear extensions may remain compatible
with the same retained edges. Aggregated preference graphs are generally
not acyclic, as combining partially disagreeing sources routinely produces
directed cycles. The combinatorial problem of removing the minimum-weight
set of edges required to make a weighted directed graph acyclic is the
(minimum-weight) feedback arc set problem. This problem is NP-hard in
general~\cite{karp1972reducibility}. Classical work provides fast,
deterministic greedy heuristics that iteratively remove the lightest edge on
a discovered cycle~\cite{eades1993heuristic}, while later work offers
polynomial-time approximation schemes and connects the problem to
correlation clustering~\cite{kenyon2007fewerrors,ailon2008aggregating}.
Exact approaches are often formulated via the related linear-ordering
problem using branch-and-cut or cutting-plane
methods~\cite{groetschel1984cutting,baharev2021exact}. This paper does not
contribute a new formulation for the minimum-weight feedback-arc-set
problem, a classical topic in combinatorial optimization. Instead, it
studies the empirical consequences of solving this problem—both
heuristically and to certified optimality—for a downstream retrieval task.

The same structural issue has recently reappeared in the context of large
language models used as pairwise or comparative judges: repeated or
multi-evaluator LLM comparisons frequently produce transitivity violations
and directed cycles, which several recent studies diagnose or attempt to
resolve~\cite{zheng2023judging,shi2025judging,hu2026acyclic}. That work
generally treats acyclicity as a desirable property and reports
downstream gains on various tasks~\cite{hu2026acyclic}. However, it does not
test whether optimizing for acyclicity is diagnostic of retrieval
effectiveness under a corrected, paired statistical protocol, which is the
specific gap this paper addresses.

This paper substantially extends and revises an earlier preprint by the same
author~\cite{vahidi2026consistencyaware}. The preprint studied a smaller,
partially overlapping evidence base under a less conservative evaluation
protocol and reported a conditional positive retrieval effect. That preprint
has not completed peer review at its original target venue and is cited here
only as a preprint, not as a prior peer-reviewed publication. The present
study is not a resubmission. It employs a more conservative statistical
protocol, adds exact repair to rule out heuristic artifacts, and reaches a
different, more restrained conclusion on an updated evidence base. Readers
should treat the two as distinct, non-comparable analyses rather than as an
earlier and later version of the same result.

\subsection{Structural Quality Versus Downstream Utility}
\label{sec:related-utility}

A recurring pattern in the literature is that structural quality metrics
(e.g., cyclicity, removed feedback-arc weight) are reported but rarely
tested directly against a downstream task metric under corrected statistical
inference. This mirrors a broader distinction in the data-quality literature
between \emph{intrinsic} quality (properties of the data itself) and
\emph{contextual} quality (fitness for a specific
use)~\cite{wang1996beyond}. Work on data cascades and benchmark label errors
shows that upstream choices can shape downstream conclusions in ways that
are missed when only the final task metric is
inspected~\cite{sambasivan2021datacascades,northcutt2021labelerrors}. Simple
significance testing is also an unreliable guide to retrieval system
comparisons unless multiplicity and pairing are handled
correctly~\cite{urbano2019statistical,carterette2012multiple,ihemelandu2024multiple}.
Furthermore, the interpretation of a non-significant result requires care
distinct from ordinary significance
testing~\cite{lakens2018equivalence}.

Few studies in the preference-graph or feedback-arc-set literature isolate
structural improvement from downstream task utility as two separately
measured and tested quantities. Most studies either report only structural
diagnostics or report a task metric without a control for repair-method
suboptimality, such as exact repair, which would rule it out as an
explanation for a null or weak downstream effect. The controlled separation
employed in this study—explicit construction choices, comparison of
unrepaired, heuristic, and exact repair, and multiplicity-corrected paired
testing—is designed to close this specific gap
(Section~\ref{sec:introduction}).

\section{Background and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:background}

This section formalizes the four stages of the pipeline introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}: retrieval setting, preference graph
derivation, structural inconsistency, and repair. It then discusses ranking
extraction and evaluation. The definitions are stated with sufficient
generality to frame the research question, while the specific instantiation
of this framework is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}.

\subsection{Retrieval and Candidate Rankings}
\label{sec:retrieval-setting}

Let $q \in \mathcal{Q}$ denote a query drawn from a fixed query set, and let
$D_q = \{d_1,\dots,d_n\}$ denote its query-specific candidate document set:
the pool of documents that will be compared and ranked for $q$. Candidate
sets are formed upstream of our pipeline, typically from the union
of documents scored by one or more base rankers, and are held fixed.

A base ranker $s \in S$ assigns a score $x_{q,s}(d) \in \mathbb{R}$ to each
document $d \in D_q$ it evaluates. Scores may be missing for some documents.
Because raw scores from different rankers are not directly comparable, they
are normalized per query and per ranker before cross-ranker comparison. The
normalization used in the canonical experiments is per-query, per-ranker
min-max scaling,
\begin{equation}
\bar{x}_{q,s}(d) =
\frac{x_{q,s}(d) - \min_{d' \in D_{q,s}} x_{q,s}(d')}
     {\max_{d' \in D_{q,s}} x_{q,s}(d') - \min_{d' \in D_{q,s}} x_{q,s}(d')},
\label{eq:minmax}
\end{equation}
where $D_{q,s}\subseteq D_q$ is the subset of candidates scored by ranker $s$
for query $q$. If all retained scores for a query--ranker pair are equal
(range below $10^{-12}$), the normalized vector is set to all zeros. Missing
candidates are excluded from the range calculation and remain abstentions
when votes are formed. Pairwise votes are built from normalized scores
$\bar{x}_{q,s}$ (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}). The unnormalized construction
is used only for a robustness ablation, as raw score margins are not a
scale-neutral basis for comparing rankers.

Relevance judgments (qrels), which provide graded or binary labels for
query-document pairs, are drawn from external human annotations and are not
derived from ranker scores or preference graphs. All retrieval metrics are
computed against these external judgments at a fixed cutoff $k$. For
example, nDCG$@k$ scores only the top-$k$ documents in a ranking. The
relationship between the candidate pool size $P = |D_q|$ and the cutoff $k$
is an explicit design choice (Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}). Repair
can change the top-$k$ set only when $P > k$.

\subsection{Derived Weighted Preference Graph}
\label{sec:pref-graph}

For each query $q$, the normalized ranker scores $\bar{x}_{q,s}$ are
converted into a weighted directed preference graph
\begin{equation}
G_q = (V_q, E_q, w_q), \qquad V_q = D_q,
\label{eq:graph-def}
\end{equation}
where a directed edge $(u,v) \in E_q$ with weight $w_q(u,v) > 0$ represents
the strength of evidence for ranking document $u$ ahead of document $v$. The
graph is a derived object, not ground truth. Its structure (edges,
directions, and weights) is determined by an explicit construction
procedure applied to the normalized scores, not by intrinsic properties of
the documents.

Construction proceeds ranker by ranker and pair by pair. For each ranker $s$
and each scored document pair $\{u,v\} \subseteq D_q$, a directional vote
($u \succ v$ or $v \succ u$) is cast based on the normalized scores. The vote
strength is derived from the normalized score margin,
$|\bar{x}_{q,s}(u) - \bar{x}_{q,s}(v)|$. Ties in normalized score or pairs
where a document is unscored result in an abstention. These are treated as
an absence of evidence, not evidence of indifference. Votes are aggregated
per directed pair. A retention rule, the \emph{vote-construction regime},
then determines which pairs are retained as edges in $G_q$.

This study evaluates three vote-construction regimes, varying only the
retention rule while holding the candidate set and ranker scores fixed:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{\texttt{ms2}} (conservative): retain a direction only with
    majority support from rankers and an aggregated margin above a
    threshold. This conservative regime suppresses most two-way
    contradictions and typically produces acyclic graphs.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{ms1}} (permissive): retain every ranker-supported
    directional vote. This permissive regime allows for direct mutual
    contradictions—where both $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$ are retained—and thus
    longer cycles.
  \item \textbf{\texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}}: similar to \texttt{ms1}, but
    drops pairs with bidirectional support during vote construction. This
    isolates cyclicity arising from longer-cycle nontransitivity rather
    than direct mutual contradictions. It is distinct from a post-hoc
    modification of an \texttt{ms1} graph.
\end{itemize}
These regimes are not proposed as optimal construction rules but serve as a
controlled sweep to establish graph construction as a first-class
experimental factor. Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup} reports its strong
effect on graph cyclicity. Since the vote-construction regime determines
the level of inconsistency, any observed repair effect must be interpreted
in the context of the regime that produced the graph.

\subsection{Cycles and Structural Inconsistency}
\label{sec:cycles}

A weighted directed graph $G_q$ is \emph{acyclic} if it contains no directed
cycle $u_1 \to u_2 \to \dots \to u_m \to u_1$; equivalently, its strongly
connected components are all singletons. An acyclic graph $G_q$ admits at
least one consistent linear extension. Conversely, a cyclic graph cannot be
represented by any single total order over $D_q$ without violating at least
one edge.

We report two specific measures of structural inconsistency rather than a
single omnibus cyclicity score. The first is the cyclic-query rate: the
fraction of queries for which $G_q$ is cyclic under a given construction
regime. The second measure distinguishes cyclicity from direct mutual
contradictions (i.e., pairs retained in both directions) from that which
persists after their removal. We report two related diagnostics:
post-aggregation deletion of reciprocal edges from an \texttt{ms1} graph,
and the separate \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual} construction. These are not
guaranteed to coincide because they handle mutual contradictions at
different stages and may use different thresholds
(Section~\ref{sec:results-structure}). Conflating them would misrepresent
the source of cyclicity.

Acyclicity is a purely structural property. It guarantees the existence of a
consistent total order but does not guarantee the correctness of the
retained edges, agreement with relevance judgments, or superior performance
of any particular ranking extraction rule. We distinguish between
transitivity, acyclicity, evidential correctness, and retrieval optimality.
Accordingly, Sections~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair} and~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}
separate the repair objective (feedback-arc weight) from the evaluation
metric (nDCG).

\subsection{Minimum-Weight Feedback-Arc-Set Repair}
\label{sec:mwfas-repair}

Given a preference graph $G_q = (V_q, E_q, w_q)$, repair seeks a
minimum-weight feedback arc set $F_q^\star \subseteq E_q$ whose removal
renders the graph acyclic:
\begin{equation}
F_q^\star = \arg\min_{F \subseteq E_q} \sum_{e \in F} w_q(e)
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad (V_q,\, E_q \setminus F) \text{ is acyclic}.
\label{eq:mwfas}
\end{equation}
This is the minimum-weight feedback arc set (MWFAS) problem
(Section~\ref{sec:related-repair}); the repaired graph is
$\widetilde{G}_q = (V_q, E_q \setminus F_q^\star, w_q)$. The objective in
Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas} is purely structural: it minimizes disagreement
with the graph's own edges to achieve acyclicity. It makes no reference to
external relevance judgments or retrieval utility, a distinction central to
our research question.

We evaluate two co-equal solution methods for Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}.
\textbf{Greedy repair} is a fast heuristic that iteratively finds a cycle
and removes its minimum-weight edge until the graph is acyclic. This
heuristic is not guaranteed to be optimal but is computationally efficient.
\textbf{Exact repair} solves Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas} to certified
optimality using a mixed-integer program based on the equivalent
linear-ordering problem~\cite{groetschel1984cutting,baharev2021exact}. This
involves finding a total order of the nodes that minimizes the weight of
backward-directed edges.
\begin{equation}
\min \sum_{(u,v) \in E_q} w_q(u,v)\, \mathrm{before}[v,u],
\label{eq:mwfas-mip}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the total weight of retained edges oriented against the order. The
removed set is exactly those backward edges, so the objective equals the
MWFAS objective in Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}; every acyclic retained subgraph
admits at least one topological ordering, and the MIP searches over all total
orders. We use the open-source SCIP solver and include a graph in the
exact-repair analysis only if the solver certifies optimality.

Exact repair serves as a \emph{diagnostic control}, not a practical
replacement for the greedy heuristic. We make no claims about its general
scalability. Its role is specific to our research question. By construction,
the solution $F_q^\star$ from exact repair is a global optimum of
Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}; no method can restore acyclicity by removing less
edge weight. If the retrieval effect of exact repair is statistically
indistinguishable from that of greedy repair, then the heuristic's
suboptimality cannot explain a null or weak retrieval effect. This addresses
an objection that a greedy-only study cannot.

\subsection{Ranking Extraction and Evaluation}
\label{sec:extraction-eval}

A final ranking is extracted from the unrepaired graph $G_q$, the repaired
graph $\widetilde{G}_q$, or the upstream ranker signals alone. The canonical
experiments compare four families of extraction rules. Graph-dependent rules
are evaluated on both unrepaired and repaired graphs:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Prior-only}: ranks by an upstream per-document score,
    independent of the graph.
  \item \textbf{Graph-free fusion}: methods such as reciprocal rank fusion,
    CombSUM, and Borda-style fusion, which operate on the constituent
    rankers' outputs without constructing a preference graph.
  \item \textbf{Graph-only extraction}: a Copeland-style score
    $s_{\mathrm{cop}}(u) = \deg^+(u) - \deg^-(u)$ (unweighted
    out-degree minus in-degree); a weighted balance score
    $s_{\mathrm{bal}}(u) = \sum_{(u,v) \in E} w(u,v) - \sum_{(v,u) \in E}
    w(v,u)$ (weighted out-edge mass minus in-edge mass); and one or more
    stationary-distribution scores computed from a Markov chain over the
    (weighted) graph, in the spirit of Rank Centrality
    (Section~\ref{sec:related-pairwise}). Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}
    details the specific instantiations of these rules, which include
    multiple Markov-chain variants and other centrality measures. Each rule
    is applied to both the unrepaired ($G_q$) and repaired
    ($\widetilde{G}_q$) graphs, holding the rule constant to isolate the
    effect of repair.
  \item \textbf{Hybrid prior-plus-graph extraction}: the graph score is
    combined with a graph-free prior score,
    \begin{equation}
    h_q(d) = \hat{s}^{\mathrm{prior}}_q(d)
             + \alpha\, \hat{s}^{\mathrm{graph}}_q(d),
    \label{eq:hybrid}
    \end{equation}
    where both score components are independently min-max normalized per
    query. The primary analysis uses $\alpha=0.3$, with other values tested
    for robustness. The prior $\hat{s}^{\mathrm{prior}}_q$ is reciprocal
    rank fusion over the three base rankers on $D_q$ with $k{=}60$, not a
    simple score sum; final ties break by document id. The method is
    unrepaired when $\hat{s}^{\mathrm{graph}}_q$ is computed from $G_q$ and
    repaired when computed from $\widetilde{G}_q$.
\end{itemize}

Retrieval effectiveness is measured with normalized discounted cumulative
gain (nDCG), the standard graded-relevance ranking
metric~\cite{jarvelin2002cumulated}: for a ranking with relevance labels
$\mathrm{rel}_1,\dots,\mathrm{rel}_k$ at ranks $1,\dots,k$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{\mathrm{rel}_i}-1}{\log_2(i+1)},
\qquad
\mathrm{nDCG}@k = \frac{\mathrm{DCG}@k}{\mathrm{IDCG}@k},
\label{eq:ndcg}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{IDCG}@k$ is the DCG of the ideal ranking. Thus,
$\mathrm{nDCG}@k \in [0,1]$, with higher values indicating better agreement
with relevance judgments. We compute nDCG per query and compare the
unrepaired and repaired variants of each extraction rule while holding all
other factors constant. Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup} specifies the
paired, multiplicity-corrected statistical protocol used to test for
significant differences.

This formulation clarifies why optimizing the repair objective
(Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}) is not equivalent to improving nDCG. The repair
objective depends only on the graph's internal structure, which is derived
from upstream choices (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}). In contrast, nDCG is
computed against external relevance judgments that are absent from the
repair objective. A smaller feedback-arc weight, even if proven optimal, is
evidence of internal consistency, not necessarily of agreement with external
relevance judgments. Sections~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}
and~\ref{sec:results} investigate whether an empirical link exists between
these two objectives.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

Section~\ref{sec:background} formalized the objects this study reasons
about -- candidate rankings, the derived preference graph, structural
inconsistency, MWFAS repair, and ranking extraction -- at the level of
generality needed to state the paper's research question precisely. This
section instantiates that formulation: the specific research questions it
answers, the concrete pipeline, datasets, rankers, construction protocol,
repair implementations, extraction methods, baselines, metrics, and
statistical-inference procedure used to produce the evidence reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}. No outcome values are reported here; this
section documents what was measured and how, not what was found.

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

The study answers four questions that operationalize the separation of
construction, structural repair, and retrieval utility
(Section~\ref{sec:introduction}):

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How much structural inconsistency -- cyclicity and
    contradictory edge mass -- is present in derived multi-ranker
    preference graphs, and how strongly does it depend on graph-construction
    choices (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}) rather than on the underlying
    documents?
  \item[RQ2] How do greedy and exact minimum-weight feedback-arc-set repair
    (Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}) differ in the structural change they
    produce -- removed edge weight, the chosen feedback arc set, and
    top-$k$ membership when the candidate pool exceeds the evaluation
    cutoff -- on the same graphs?
  \item[RQ3] Does preference-graph repair, heuristic or exact, produce a
    statistically reliable improvement in downstream retrieval
    effectiveness (nDCG) relative to the unrepaired graph, once paired
    inference and multiplicity correction are applied -- and how do
    repaired graph methods compare with simple graph-free fusion anchors?
  \item[RQ4] Is any observed retrieval null in RQ3 attributable to
    greedy-repair suboptimality rather than to repair itself, once exact
    MWFAS is used as a diagnostic control under matched construction,
    pooling, and extraction choices?
\end{description}

These questions concern the marginal effect of repair under the stated
protocol. They do not ask for a learned per-query policy that decides when
to invoke repair, nor for a state-of-the-art reranker comparison beyond the
graph-free fusion anchors used to contextualize that marginal effect.

\subsection{Experimental Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Every reported result is produced by the same nine-stage pipeline, run
once per query under each combination of dataset, vote-construction
regime, and evaluation cell studied:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item obtain stored per-document scores from each base ranker
    (Section~\ref{sec:rankers}) for a fixed candidate pool;
  \item normalize scores per query and per ranker
    (Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax});
  \item derive weighted pairwise votes from normalized score margins
    (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph});
  \item construct the query-specific weighted directed preference graph
    $G_q$ under one vote-construction regime (Section~\ref{sec:construction-protocol});
  \item extract unrepaired rankings directly from $G_q$ and from the prior
    score alone;
  \item repair $G_q$ into $\widetilde{G}_q$ with greedy cycle-peeling or
    exact SCIP-based MWFAS solving (Section~\ref{sec:repair-protocol});
  \item extract repaired rankings from $\widetilde{G}_q$ using the same
    extraction rule applied to $G_q$ (Section~\ref{sec:extraction-methods});
  \item evaluate every extracted ranking against relevance judgments with
    nDCG at a prespecified cutoff (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}); and
  \item conduct paired, multiplicity-corrected statistical inference on
    repaired-versus-unrepaired differences (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}).
\end{enumerate}
Stages 1--4 are shared by every downstream comparison; stages 5--9 are
repeated once per extraction rule and once per repair method (unrepaired,
greedy, exact) being compared. No stage regenerates upstream retrieval:
stage 1 reuses fixed, versioned score files, so every reported statistic
is reproducible from stored intermediates without repeating retrieval or
querying a paid API (Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}).

\subsection{Datasets and Retrieval Tasks}
\label{sec:datasets}

The study uses four public retrieval benchmarks, chosen to span distinct
retrieval domains so that construction-sensitivity and repair findings can
be checked for domain generality rather than being tied to one corpus
type: SciDocs, a scientific-document retrieval benchmark built around
citation-informed document representations~\cite{cohan2020scidocs}; FiQA,
a financial-opinion question-answering and retrieval
benchmark~\cite{maia2018fiqa}; HotpotQA, a multi-hop question-answering

\subsection{Base Rankers and Judgment Sources}
\label{sec:rankers}

Three base rankers provide heterogeneous ranking signals for the
preference-aggregation study; they are a controlled substrate for studying
construction and repair, not a claim of retrieval state of the art.
\textbf{BM25} scores each candidate with the standard Okapi BM25 term-weighting
formula~\cite{robertson2009probabilistic}, using the conventional parameter
values $k_1=1.5$, $b=0.75$. \textbf{TF-IDF} scores candidates by cosine
similarity between query and document term-frequency--inverse-document-frequency
vectors under standard logarithmic term-frequency weighting~\cite{salton1988termweighting}.
\textbf{MiniLM} scores candidates by cosine similarity between query and
document embeddings from the pretrained \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}
sentence-embedding model, a distilled transformer encoder~\cite{wang2020minilm}
used here as a fixed, off-the-shelf dense-similarity signal, not fine-tuned
for any dataset in this study. All three rankers are applied to the same
per-query candidate pool and produce one real-valued score per
candidate; higher scores indicate stronger estimated relevance for every
ranker, so no ranker requires a direction transformation before
normalization (Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax}).

\subsection{Preference-Graph Construction}
\label{sec:construction-protocol}

The primary construction protocol normalizes every ranker's scores with
per-query, per-ranker min-max scaling (Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax}) and
extracts directional votes from normalized score margins, following
Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}. Vote retention uses a retention-matched
thresholding rule that never consults qrels or nDCG. On each dataset, a
raw-margin (unnormalized) construction with the same support rule supplies
targets: per-ranker retained-vote rates and the regime's retained directed
edge count. For the normalized protocol, each ranker's vote-margin
threshold is the empirical quantile of that ranker's pooled pairwise
normalized margins matching the raw retained-vote rate
($q=1-\mathrm{target\_rate}$). Given those vote thresholds, the
regime-level aggregate weight threshold is chosen by a discrete search over
candidate aggregate weights to minimize the absolute gap in retained edge
count versus the raw baseline for that regime (ties break toward the smaller
threshold). Vote-margin thresholds are therefore per dataset and per
ranker, shared across regimes within a dataset; aggregate thresholds are
per dataset and per regime; neither is per query. Thresholds are fixed from
the full usable query set of that dataset before retrieval metrics are
computed. Matching is approximate (minimum absolute edge-count gap), not
exact equality of every rate. Canonical values appear in
Table~\ref{tab:thresholds}. Support requirements are fixed per regime:
\texttt{ms2} requires support from at least two of the three rankers;
\texttt{ms1} and \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual} require support from at least
one ranker, differing in whether contested (two-way) pairs are dropped at
vote construction (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}).

Candidate pools use one primary policy: for each query, the top-$P$
documents of a reciprocal-rank-fusion ranking computed over the union of
the three rankers' native (unnormalized) score lists. This ``RRF-centered''
pool policy is one specific, named choice among several equally valid
ways to truncate a candidate pool, and it determines which documents are
eligible to appear in $D_q$ at all -- a decision made once, upstream of
score normalization and graph construction. Four alternative,
independently defined pool policies (equal per-ranker depth, round-robin
interleaving across rankers, a single-ranker pool, and a CombSUM-ranked
pool) are evaluated as a robustness check (Section~\ref{sec:results}) to
test whether the study's structural and retrieval conclusions depend on
this specific choice.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:construction} summarizes graph construction for a single
query, reflecting the procedure implemented in the repository's graph- and
vote-construction code. The description is intentionally compact: the
contribution is not a new preference extractor, but a controlled study of
how these construction choices affect cyclicity, repair, and downstream
retrieval.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Preference-graph construction for one query}
\label{alg:construction}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Query $q$; candidate set $D_q$; base rankers $S=\{\mathrm{BM25},
  \mathrm{TF\text{-}IDF}, \mathrm{MiniLM}\}$ with scores $x_{q,s}$;
  construction regime $r \in \{\texttt{ms2}, \texttt{ms1},
  \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}\}$
\Ensure Weighted directed preference graph $G_q=(V_q,E_q,w_q)$
\State $V_q \gets D_q$; initialize an empty weighted edge accumulator
\For{each ranker $s \in S$}
  \State Normalize $x_{q,s}$ to $\bar{x}_{q,s}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:minmax})
  \For{each unordered candidate pair $\{u,v\} \subseteq D_q$ scored by $s$}
    \If{$|\bar{x}_{q,s}(u) - \bar{x}_{q,s}(v)|$ is below the vote-margin
      threshold, or $s$ does not score both $u$ and $v$}
      \State Abstain on $\{u,v\}$ for ranker $s$
    \Else
      \State Accumulate directional support for $u \succ v$ or $v \succ u$
        (whichever $\bar{x}_{q,s}$ supports), weighted by the normalized
        margin
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Apply regime $r$'s support rule and aggregate weight threshold to
  every accumulated directed pair
\If{$r = \texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}$}
  \State Remove every unordered pair retained in both directions
\EndIf
\State $E_q \gets$ every retained directed pair with positive aggregated weight
\State \Return $G_q = (V_q, E_q, w_q)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Repair Methods}
\label{sec:repair-protocol}

Two repair methods solve Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}, reported as co-equal
throughout; their roles are defined in Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}.
\textbf{Greedy repair} is the repository's own deterministic cycle-peeling
heuristic (not the classical Eades--Lin--Smyth linear-arrangement
heuristic~\cite{eades1993heuristic}; Section~\ref{sec:related-repair}).
\textbf{Exact repair} solves the linear-ordering mixed-integer program
(Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas-mip}) with the open-source SCIP solver via
PySCIPOpt (version 6.2.1), requiring a proven-optimal status (relative
optimality gap $0.0$) rather than accepting a feasible incumbent, with a
300-second per-query wall-clock time limit as a safety margin. Every
exact-repair result reported in this study reached proven optimality; the
time limit was never approached (observed per-query solve times range
from sub-millisecond to a few hundred milliseconds at the graph sizes
studied here). The linear-ordering formulation was independently
cross-checked against an unrelated brute-force exact method (exhaustive
permutation search) on synthetic and real query graphs, with all checked
cases matching exactly. Exact repair is retained here solely as the
diagnostic control defined in Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}, answering
RQ4 (Section~\ref{sec:results-exact}).

\subsection{Ranking Extraction Methods}
\label{sec:extraction-methods}

Extraction methods fall into four groups, extending
Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}'s general definitions with the specific
methods this study evaluates and stating precisely which methods enter
which comparison family (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\textbf{Prior-only and graph-free fusion} rank candidates without
constructing or consulting a preference graph: the prior-only method ranks
by the same RRF prior used in Equation~\eqref{eq:hybrid}; reciprocal rank
fusion, CombSUM, and Borda-style fusion combine the three rankers'
individual per-query rankings or scores
directly~\cite{cormack2009rrf,fox1994combination}.

\textbf{Graph-only extraction} computes a score from $G_q$ or
$\widetilde{G}_q$ alone. Table~\ref{tab:extraction-params} records the
implementation parameters used throughout. The Copeland score
$s_{\mathrm{cop}}$ and balance score $s_{\mathrm{bal}}$
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}) are evaluated on every dataset, regime,
and repair method. Two related but distinct Markov-chain scores are also
evaluated: a damped stationary-distribution score with uniform restart
(``Markov''), and an undamped Rank Centrality chain with explicit
self-loops~\cite{negahban2017rankcentrality}. Standard graph PageRank on
the reversed preference graph~\cite{page1999pagerank}, Bradley--Terry MM
MLE with edge weights as win
counts~\cite{bradley1952rank,hunter2004mm}, and (on repaired DAGs only)
NetworkX topological order plus prior-priority topological order complete
the set.

\textbf{Hybrid prior-plus-graph extraction} combines a graph-only score
with the RRF prior via Equation~\eqref{eq:hybrid}. Copeland and balance
hybrids use $\alpha=0.3$ in the primary analysis, with
$\alpha \in \{0.1,0.3,0.5,1.0\}$ as a robustness check; a Markov hybrid at
the same fixed $\alpha$ enters the expanded family
(Section~\ref{sec:baselines}).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The primary retrieval metric is normalized discounted cumulative gain
(nDCG) at a prespecified cutoff $k$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:ndcg}), evaluated
per query and averaged across a dataset's usable queries; higher values
indicate better agreement with graded relevance judgments. Mean
reciprocal rank (MRR) and mean average
precision (MAP) are computed as secondary diagnostics only and are never
used as a primary confirmatory metric. All three are computed at the same
evaluated $(P,k)$ cell (Table~\ref{tab:setup}) for every method and repair
variant being compared.

Following Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}, the repair objective
(Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}), structural diagnostics (cyclic-query rate,
removed-weight fraction, top-$k$ membership-change rate), and retrieval
metrics (nDCG, with MRR/MAP secondary) are never treated as proxies for
one another or averaged together.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
\label{sec:statistics}

The unit of statistical inference is the query: for a given dataset,
construction regime, evaluation cell, and extraction method, the
repaired-minus-unrepaired nDCG difference is computed once per query,
and all inference is conducted on this vector of paired per-query
differences, never on a coarser or finer unit. The primary confirmatory
test for every repaired-versus-unrepaired comparison is an exact sign-flip
permutation test on the paired differences (enumerating all $2^m$ sign
patterns when the number of nonzero differences $m \le 20$; a
$10{,}000$-replicate Monte Carlo approximation with a fixed seed
otherwise), testing the null hypothesis that the paired differences are
symmetric about zero. Every $p$-value from a reported family of cells
(e.g.\ the full canonical family, or the full larger-pool family) is
adjusted jointly with the Holm step-down procedure~\cite{holm1979simple},
controlling the family-wise error rate at $\alpha=0.05$. Families are
corrected separately (Table~\ref{tab:holm-families}): confirmatory
families answer the primary repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG question under
greedy or exact repair; robustness families vary pool, baselines, or ask
equivalence/power questions. The reported families were fixed in the
canonical evidence specification before the final manuscript analysis;
family membership was not reopened after inspecting the final Holm

Beyond this primary test, four robustness procedures are reported for

\subsection{Structural Effect of Repair}
\label{sec:results-repair-structure}

Repair is structurally active precisely where Section~\ref{sec:results-structure}
shows a graph has contradictory structure to remove, and inert elsewhere.
Under \texttt{ms2}, repair removes nothing on any query, because no graph
is cyclic. Under \texttt{ms1}, the active regime, repair removes a
mean of $7.4\%$ (SciDocs) to $8.0\%$ (FiQA) of total graph weight; under
\texttt{ms1\_drop\_mutual}, the fraction still to remove after mutual
pairs are already gone drops to $0.2\%$--$0.4\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:structural-outcomes}).
This confirms that graph construction, not the repair algorithm, is what
decides whether repair has any opportunity to act at all -- a point kept
separate here from whether that activity translates into a retrieval
effect, which Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval} addresses directly.

Repair also changes which documents are visible for evaluation once the
candidate pool exceeds the metric cutoff. At $P=k$, top-$k$ set membership
cannot change by construction: the evaluated prefix is the entire pool, so
repairing the graph can reorder documents but never add or remove one from
the top-$k$ set, and the measured membership-change rate is exactly
$0\%$. Once $P>k$, pooling across all three vote-construction regimes and
every larger-pool evaluation cell (Section~\ref{sec:datasets}), the mean
top-$k$ membership-change rate rises to $10.6\%$. Repair is therefore not
merely reordering an already-fixed prefix under $P>k$: it can and does
change which documents are evaluated at all. Whether that structural
activity is accompanied by a reliable retrieval-effectiveness gain is
addressed next.

\subsection{Exact Versus Heuristic Repair}
\label{sec:results-exact}

The comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval} use greedy repair,
which does not generally reach the optimum of the feedback-arc-set
objective (Equation~\eqref{eq:mwfas}). This subsection reports what
changes when repair is instead solved to certified optimality, addressing
RQ4 directly: whether the retrieval null in
Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval} could instead reflect greedy repair's
suboptimality.

Of the $1{,}026$ nominal query--regime combinations
($342$ usable queries $\times$ three vote-construction regimes), one
BRIGHT query under \texttt{ms2} (\texttt{biology:0}) produces an empty
edge set -- no directed pair meets \texttt{ms2}'s min-support requirement
of two rankers -- and is excluded from the exact-repair study, leaving
$1{,}025$ valid exact-repair instances. Every one of those solves reached
proven optimality: $1{,}025/1{,}025$ query graphs, relative optimality gap
$0.0$, with a mean solve time of $7.4$ms (median $0.25$ms, maximum
$236$ms) against a $300$-second per-query safety limit that was never
approached (Section~\ref{sec:repair-protocol}). Restricted to the $379$
queries (pooled across all three vote-construction regimes) whose raw
graph is actually cyclic, exact repair finds a different feedback arc set
than greedy in $87.9\%$ of them, and removes systematically less total
edge weight: Table~\ref{tab:exact-vs-greedy} and Figure~\ref{fig:exact-vs-greedy}
report the per-dataset gap. Greedy cycle-peeling is therefore a real,
non-trivial overestimate of the minimum weighted feedback arc set on this
evidence, not merely a theoretical possibility.

reveal a retrieval gain of the scale detectable under this protocol,
greedy under-repair is ruled out as an explanation for a retrieval gain
of that scale in Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval}.

\subsection{Supporting LLM Evidence}
\label{sec:results-llm}

A bounded pilot supplements the score-derived evidence above with real
large-language-model pairwise judgments: six queries, four providers, and
four preference-graph construction variants, analyzed at the query-cluster
level ($n=6$ independent clusters), since the pilot's replicated rows
within a query are repeated measurements, not additional independent
samples. Whole-graph repair's effect on retrieval, estimated the same way
as the score-derived comparisons above, is a mean nDCG change of
$0.00019$ with a cluster-level $95\%$ interval of $[-0.00072, 0.00140]$
(exact sign-flip $p=0.875$): indistinguishable from zero, directionally
consistent with the null finding in Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval}.
Six clusters give this pilot very limited power, so this result is
reported as directional and supporting only -- it does not, on its own,
extend any conclusion in this paper beyond the four benchmarks and
construction regimes making up the main evidence package
(Section~\ref{sec:datasets}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Main Findings}
\label{sec:discussion-main}

Taken together, the results answer the research questions with one
restrained conclusion: under the canonical protocol, preference-graph
repair is a real structural intervention -- including under exact
optimization -- but not a validated general means of improving retrieval
effectiveness (Sections~\ref{sec:results-structure}--\ref{sec:results-exact}).
Structural consistency and downstream retrieval utility behave here as
empirically distinct objectives, not as the same quantity observed at
different resolutions.

That claim is narrower than it may first appear. It does not say that
graph repair never helps retrieval, that acyclicity is useless, that exact
repair always fails to improve rankings, or that the null of no effect has
been proven. A null under corrected inference is evidence against a
general, reliably detectable effect at the scale this study is powered to
observe (Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}); small or dataset-specific
effects below that scale remain possible. On the evidence gathered here,
treating acyclicity as a surrogate for retrieval effectiveness is not
supported.

\subsection{Why Structural Improvement Need Not Improve Retrieval}
\label{sec:discussion-mechanism}

Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval} already states the formal reason a
repair-objective improvement need not track nDCG: the two objectives are
computed from different information. This subsection interprets that gap
in light of the results, distinguishing what the experiments establish
from mechanisms consistent with -- but not directly tested by -- the
design.

Several mechanisms are consistent with genuine structural repair producing
no detectable retrieval effect, without any one of them being individually
isolated. Feedback-arc-set repair targets the least-weight edges needed to
restore acyclicity (Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}); those need not be the
edges that separate highly relevant from marginally relevant documents
near the top of a ranking. A cycle broken far from top-ranked positions
can leave the extracted ordering of the most relevant documents unchanged.
Where a hybrid mixes a graph-derived score with a prior
(Equation~\eqref{eq:hybrid}), an informative prior can also compress the
influence of the graph-side score -- consistent with graph-only and hybrid
methods not diverging sharply in Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval}.
Different extraction rules translate the same repaired graph differently
(Section~\ref{sec:extraction-methods}), which is one plausible contributor
to the mixed-sign point estimates in Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap-forest}.
Finally, pairwise preference weights and relevance judgments are built
from different evidence (Sections~\ref{sec:pref-graph},
\ref{sec:retrieval-setting}), so internal consistency and agreement with
qrels are free to vary independently.

None of these mechanisms is demonstrated as \emph{the} cause of the null
retrieval result; they are offered as accounts consistent with the design
and the observed pattern, not as claims established by a dedicated causal
test.

\subsection{Exact Repair as a Diagnostic Control}
\label{sec:discussion-exact}

A heuristic-only repair study leaves open the alternative that a null
retrieval result reflects under-repair rather than repair itself.
Section~\ref{sec:results-exact} closes that alternative for this evidence:
exact repair removes less edge weight than greedy yet still yields no
Holm-surviving retrieval gain against the unrepaired graph. Exact SCIP
repair is therefore used here as methodological control
(Section~\ref{sec:mwfas-repair}), not as a novel solver, a novel
formulation, or a recommendation that exact MWFAS be run at production
scale (Section~\ref{sec:related-repair}).

\subsection{Relation to Prior Literature}
\label{sec:discussion-literature}

The finding that graph repair is structurally active but not reliably
retrieval-improving sits against three adjacent, and non-contradictory,
strands of prior evidence. First, pairwise comparison signals genuinely
improve ranking quality in several settings: pairwise ranking prompting
with large language models is competitive with, and in some settings
better than, pointwise scoring for text
reranking~\cite{qin-etal-2024-large}, and pointwise, pairwise, and
listwise LLM reranking paradigms show real, measured effectiveness
differences depending on formulation~\cite{sun-etal-2023-chatgpt}. Those
findings are about whether \emph{using} pairwise evidence, instead of
pointwise scores, changes ranking effectiveness -- a different question
from this paper's, which holds the pairwise evidence source fixed and
asks whether \emph{repairing} contradictions within that evidence changes
effectiveness further. Nothing in this paper's results is in tension with
pairwise comparison being an effective ranking signal; the null result is
specifically about the marginal effect of consistency repair on top of an
already-constructed pairwise-derived graph.

Second, prior work documents that pairwise judgments, including
LLM-generated ones, are prone to inconsistency and order sensitivity:
positional and prompt-order biases can affect LLM-as-judge
outcomes~\cite{zheng2023judging}, systematic position bias has been
measured directly in LLM-as-judge pairwise
comparisons~\cite{shi2025judging}, and intrinsic inconsistency in
LLM-based pairwise rankings has motivated explicit aggregation methods to
mitigate it~\cite{zeng2026llmrankfusion}. This literature establishes that
inconsistency is a real, measurable property of pairwise preference
data, which is the premise this paper's construction-sensitivity results
(Section~\ref{sec:results-structure}) are consistent with. It does not by
itself establish that \emph{removing} that inconsistency improves a
downstream task metric; that is the further, separate claim this paper
tests directly.

Third, and closest to this paper's specific question, recent work on
acyclic preference evaluation for language models treats acyclicity as a
desirable property of an aggregated evaluation and reports downstream
gains from enforcing it, on tasks including model ranking, response
selection, and fine-tuning data
selection~\cite{hu2026acyclic}. That result is not contradicted by this
paper: the two studies test acyclicity's downstream value in different
task settings (LLM evaluation and data selection versus document
retrieval) with different aggregation and repair mechanisms, so a
positive effect in one setting and an unsupported general effect in
another are both consistent with the same underlying fact -- that whether
structural consistency helps a downstream task is an empirical property
of the specific task and pipeline, not a property of acyclicity itself
that transfers automatically across settings. The controlled separation
used here for retrieval is stated in Section~\ref{sec:related-utility}.

Relative to the earlier preprint discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:related-repair}, the present study is retrieval-focused
and more conservative: it adds exact repair as a diagnostic control and
applies multiplicity-corrected paired inference throughout. The two should
not be read as successive measurements of the same effect; the preprint's
specific numerical findings are superseded by this protocol, not confirmed
by it.

\subsection{Practical Implications}
\label{sec:discussion-practical}

The evidence in this paper supports a small number of restrained,
directly evidence-backed recommendations for researchers and
practitioners building preference-graph pipelines.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Do not assume an acyclic or less-cyclic preference graph is a
    better retrieval input without checking; this study's evidence does
    not support that assumption as a general rule.
  \item Evaluate repaired and unrepaired variants on the downstream
    task metric directly, with an unrepaired baseline always included as
    a comparison point, rather than reporting only structural diagnostics
    (cyclicity, removed weight) as if they were evidence of downstream
    benefit.
  \item Where a heuristic repair method is used and a null or weak
    downstream effect is observed, an exact solve on a representative
    subset of instances is an effective and specific check on whether
    heuristic suboptimality, rather than repair itself, could explain the
    result -- this is a diagnostic use of exact solving, not a claim that
    exact repair must be run at full scale.
  \item Report graph-construction choices (normalization, vote
    semantics, candidate pooling), extraction rule, and evaluation cutoff
    explicitly alongside any repair claim: Section~\ref{sec:results-structure}
    shows these choices, not the repair algorithm, are what determine
    whether a graph has any cyclic structure for repair to act on in the
    first place.
  \item Treat structural-quality metrics and downstream-utility metrics
    as separate reporting dimensions with separate evidence, not as
    interchangeable indicators of pipeline quality.
\end{itemize}

These recommendations are audit practices, not a validated deployment
rule for deciding when to apply repair. This paper does not establish,
and does not attempt to establish, a criterion for predicting per-query
whether repair will help; that is a distinct research question this
paper's evidence base does not address.

\subsection{Implications for LLM-Based Ranking}
\label{sec:discussion-llm}

Pairwise judgments from large language models can produce cyclic
preference graphs for the same structural reasons as score-derived votes
(Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}). The distinction between structural
consistency and downstream utility therefore applies to that setting in
principle, but this paper's own LLM evidence is only the bounded
six-query pilot in Section~\ref{sec:results-llm} and does not support
broad claims about modern LLM rerankers or production-scale pairwise
pipelines. Extending the controlled comparison to a larger real-LLM
evidence base remains future work.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Internal Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-internal}

The pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}) makes several sequential design
choices -- score normalization, vote-margin and aggregate-weight
thresholds, candidate pooling, and the hybrid mixing weight $\alpha$ --
each of which could in principle interact with the repair effect being
measured. Two controls mitigate this: the retention-matched thresholding
procedure (Section~\ref{sec:construction-protocol}) ties the normalized
protocol's permissiveness to the raw-margin baseline rather than choosing
it freely, and every retrieval comparison holds construction, pooling,
and extraction fixed while varying only the repair method
(Section~\ref{sec:results-retrieval}), so a measured repaired-versus-unrepaired
difference cannot be attributed to a confound in those other choices.
What remains unresolved is $\alpha$-dependence beyond the sweep reported
as a robustness check (Section~\ref{sec:extraction-methods}): the primary
analysis fixes $\alpha=0.3$, and while the sweep does not change the
qualitative conclusion, it does not exhaustively rule out every possible
mixing weight. The paired sign-flip and Holm-correction procedure
(Section~\ref{sec:statistics}) assumes exchangeability of paired
differences under the null and independence across queries within a
family; the query-level pairing is designed to satisfy the first
assumption, and queries are drawn from disjoint benchmark query sets to
support the second, but formal independence across the three
vote-construction regimes evaluated on the same underlying documents
(Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}) is not separately tested, since the
canonical and larger-pool families are never jointly corrected across
regimes for exactly this reason (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}).

\subsection{Construct Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-construct}

Acyclicity is one specific, narrow notion of structural consistency
(Section~\ref{sec:cycles}); it is not the only property a preference
graph could be judged by, and this study's structural diagnostics
(cyclic-query rate, removed feedback-arc weight, top-$k$
membership-change rate) are proxies for graph-internal coherence, not
direct measurements of evidential correctness. nDCG is this study's
primary utility construct; it captures rank-weighted agreement with
graded relevance judgments, but it is one operationalization of retrieval
quality among several (Section~\ref{sec:metrics} reports MRR and MAP only
as secondary diagnostics, precisely because the primary claim should not
rest on whichever metric happens to be most favorable). A further
construct gap, not resolved by this study's design, is that pairwise
vote weight (Section~\ref{sec:pref-graph}) encodes a ranker's confidence
in a relative ordering, not a judgment of either candidate's relevance;
the two constructs are related in that both derive from the same
underlying documents, but they are not the same measurement, and this
study does not claim the graph's internal weights are a proxy for
relevance at any point (Section~\ref{sec:extraction-eval}'s closing
paragraph states this distinction as a matter of definition, not as an
empirical finding requiring separate validation here).

\subsection{External Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-external}

The main evidence base spans four benchmarks, two lexical base rankers,
one dense base ranker, and three vote-construction regimes
(Sections~\ref{sec:datasets}, \ref{sec:rankers}) -- a controlled, not
exhaustive, sweep. It does not by itself establish generalization beyond
that set. The real-LLM pilot remains the six-query addendum of
Section~\ref{sec:results-llm} and is not an independent confirmation.
This study does not evaluate learned fusion, cross-encoder reranking,
modern dense rerankers beyond the MiniLM checkpoint used here, or online
interactive retrieval; whether structural repair behaves differently under
those settings is unaddressed. Extension to related preference-aggregation
domains (recommendation, pairwise LLM evaluation, crowdsourced preferences)
is plausible in principle (Section~\ref{sec:discussion-llm}) but untested.

\subsection{Statistical Conclusion Validity}
\label{sec:limitations-statistical}

Every confirmatory claim uses Holm-corrected paired inference over a
reported family (Section~\ref{sec:statistics}). As reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results-robustness}, the study is well positioned to
rule out a general effect at the scale of the observed point estimates,
but smaller effects below the corrected minimum-detectable scale are not
ruled out; a non-significant result does not establish equality between
repaired and unrepaired rankings, and narrow equivalence was established
only for a minority of cells. The real-LLM pilot uses cluster-level
($n=6$) inference for the reason stated in
Section~\ref{sec:statistics}; that correction does not enlarge the pilot
into a confirmatory study.

\subsection{Computational Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations-computational}

Exact MWFAS solving reaches certified optimality quickly at the
query-graph sizes studied here (Section~\ref{sec:results-exact}), but this
study does not evaluate larger graphs and makes no scalability claim beyond
those sizes. Exact repair's role remains the diagnostic control of
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-exact}. Greedy cycle-peeling remains the
practical choice for larger graphs because exact MWFAS is NP-hard in
general (Section~\ref{sec:related-repair}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

By separating graph construction, structural repair, ranking extraction,
and retrieval evaluation, this study shows that construction choices
decide whether preference graphs are cyclic at all; that greedy and exact
repair both restore acyclicity where structure exists, with exact repair
removing less weight than greedy; and that neither repair method yields a
Holm-surviving repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG gain in the canonical,
larger-pool, or exact-repair families -- even when larger-pool repair
changes top-$k$ membership. Exact repair closes greedy under-repair as an
explanation for a gain of the detectable scale without revealing a hidden
retrieval gain; equivalence evidence remains limited to a minority of
TOST cells, and smaller effects below the corrected minimum-detectable
scale remain possible.

The practical implication is not that acyclicity is irrelevant or that
repair can never help. It is that structural improvement and retrieval
improvement are different claims requiring different evidence: report
construction, repair diagnostics, extraction, and downstream metrics
separately, keep an unrepaired baseline, and use exact repair as a bounded
methodological control when a weak downstream result must be stress-tested
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion-practical}).

Future work should test the same separation on broader large-language-model
judgment benchmarks, additional construction and extraction strategies, and
downstream objectives beyond nDCG-based retrieval, while preserving the
discipline established here: evaluate acyclicity as a structural property
first, and as a retrieval intervention only when downstream evidence
supports that reading.

\section{Data Availability and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:data-availability}

The public benchmarks used in this study are available from their original
sources: SciDocs, FiQA, HotpotQA, and BRIGHT are cited in
Section~\ref{sec:datasets}. The code, fixed query lists, processed
intermediates, figure data, and scripts required to reproduce the reported
tables and figures are available at
\url{https://github.com/SoroushVahidi/consistency-aware-llm-rankin}. The
main-paper score-derived results are regenerated from stored ranker-score
and relevance-judgment artifacts; no paid provider call is required to
recompute those statistics. The bounded real-large-language-model pilot
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-llm} is supported by sanitized
derived artifacts and usage summaries in the repository. Raw provider
request and response payloads are not included in the repository, because
they may contain prompt/completion content and operational details that
are excluded by the project's artifact policy.
