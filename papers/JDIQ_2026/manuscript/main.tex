\begin{abstract}
Preference graphs built from multiple rankers are derived data artifacts,
not ground truth: their edges, weights, and cycles depend on how
heterogeneous scores are normalized, thresholded, and aggregated before any
repair algorithm is applied. We formalize that construction problem as a
seven-dimension data-quality audit covering provenance, calibration, vote
semantics, conflict structure, repair quality, downstream utility, and
reproducibility. We study four retrieval benchmarks (SciDocs, FiQA,
HotpotQA, and BRIGHT) with stored BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM scores. Raw
score margins are severely scale dominated: BM25's conditional mean
edge-weight share is $0.988$ under the raw construction but $0.512$ after
per-query, per-ranker normalization. That change materially alters retained
votes, cyclicity, removed edges, and several repaired-versus-unrepaired
signs. Under the primary normalized protocol, repair remains structurally
active, but no repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG cell survives Holm
correction in the canonical design, the larger-pool $P>k$ study, or direct
exact-repair checks. Larger pools confirm that repair can change top-$k$
membership, yet those membership changes still do not yield a reliable
aggregate improvement. Exact SCIP repair improves the graph objective but
does not reveal a retrieval gain that greedy repair missed. The main
contribution is therefore methodological rather than algorithmic: claims
about graph repair must report normalization, vote construction, pooling,
and evaluation cutoffs as first-class data-quality choices, because those
choices determine both the graph being repaired and the effects evaluation
can observe.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Preference graphs are attractive because they preserve disagreement among
retrieval systems rather than collapsing every score into a single fused
number. For a query-specific candidate set, a directed edge
$u \rightarrow v$ records support for ranking document $u$ ahead of $v$,
while the edge weight records how strongly that support should count. That
representation is useful only if the graph itself is trustworthy. When a
graph is built from heterogeneous BM25, TF-IDF, and neural similarity
scores, its structure depends on score comparability, tie handling, vote
thresholds, and candidate-pool design before any repair step sees it. The
graph is therefore a derived data artifact whose quality must be audited,
not assumed.

This paper studies that audit problem in the concrete setting of
feedback-arc-set repair for multi-ranker retrieval. Repair is often treated
as if a better graph-internal solution should translate into a better
retrieval ranking, yet the optimization target is structural consistency,
whereas retrieval metrics evaluate relevance against qrels. We examine the
construction pipeline that connects those two objects. Across SciDocs,
FiQA, HotpotQA, and BRIGHT, we measure how normalization, vote semantics,
candidate pooling, and the distinction between pool size $P$ and metric
cutoff $k$ change the preference graph and the apparent effect of repair.

The paper is not a new ranking algorithm. Its contribution is a data-quality
framing and a measured negative result: structural repair remains active,
but reliable repaired-versus-unrepaired retrieval gains do not survive once
construction choices, paired testing, multiplicity correction, larger-pool
evaluation, and exact repair are all checked explicitly.

The main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item We define the query-specific preference graph as a derived
    data-quality object and organize its failure modes into a compact,
    operational seven-dimension audit taxonomy.
  \item We show that raw heterogeneous score margins are not a neutral
    starting point: unnormalized construction is scale dominated, and
    normalization materially changes retained votes, cyclicity, and removed
    edges.
  \item We separate structural repair activity from downstream retrieval
    utility. Repair changes the graph and, under $P>k$, can change top-$k$
    membership, but no repaired-versus-unrepaired nDCG family in this study
    survives Holm correction.
  \item We show that the null retrieval conclusion is not a greedy-repair
    artifact, a candidate-pool artifact, or a baseline-coverage artifact:
    it persists under exact SCIP repair, larger pools, alternative pool
    policies, and added graph-ranking baselines.
  \item We distill the results into a practitioner-facing audit logic for
    future preference-graph studies and deployments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

Preference-graph methods sit between rank aggregation, probabilistic
pairwise ranking, fusion, and data-quality auditing. Rank-aggregation and
feedback-arc-set literatures study how to extract consistent orderings from
conflicting pairwise evidence~\cite{dwork2001rank,ailon2008aggregating,kemeny1959mathematics,kenyon2007fewerrors}.
Probabilistic pairwise models such as Bradley--Terry and Rank Centrality
consume the same kind of graph but optimize different principles over it
~\cite{bradley1952rank,negahban2017rankcentrality}. In all of those cases,
the graph is usually treated as a valid input rather than as an object with
its own measurable quality.

Information retrieval already has graph-free ways to combine heterogeneous
rankers, notably RRF, CombSUM, and Borda-style fusion
~\cite{cormack2009rrf,fox1994combination,lee1997analyses}. That literature
also makes the score-comparability problem explicit. Hybrid lexical-semantic
fusion is sensitive to score normalization, and one reason rank-based
methods remain attractive is that they avoid comparing incompatible raw
magnitudes directly~\cite{manmatha2002formal,bruch2023analysis}. The same
issue becomes more consequential in a weighted preference graph, where raw
margins determine both edge presence and edge weight.

Our framing is closest to data-quality work on derived analytical artifacts.
Intrinsic properties of the graph, such as cyclicity or removed feedback-arc
weight, can diverge from contextual task utility, just as intrinsic and
contextual data quality can diverge more generally~\cite{wang1996beyond}.
This derived-artifact perspective is consistent with work on data cascades
and benchmark error propagation, which shows that upstream curation choices
silently shape downstream conclusions~\cite{sambasivan2021datacascades,northcutt2021labelerrors}.
The paper operationalizes that perspective for preference graphs rather than
borrowing a general framework and applying it loosely.

Finally, recent LLM-as-judge and pairwise-ranking studies reinforce the same
measurement lesson: parser defaults, prompt order, and positional biases can
manufacture edges that are then misread as meaningful inconsistency
~\cite{sun-etal-2023-chatgpt,qin-etal-2024-large,zheng2023judging,shi2025judging,zeng2024llmrankfusion}.
We use stored LLM judgments only as a bounded protocol-quality check, not as
primary evidence, but they show that the construction-first logic is not
limited to classical rankers.

\section{Audit Framework and Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

For a query $q$ with candidate set $D_q$ and rankers
$s \in \{\mathrm{BM25}, \mathrm{TF\mbox{-}IDF}, \mathrm{MiniLM}\}$, the
audited object is the weighted directed graph
$G_q=(V_q,E_q,w_q)$ with $V_q=D_q$. It is the output of six named stages:
candidate-pool construction, score normalization, pairwise vote extraction,
abstention handling, thresholding, and edge aggregation. The graph is not
treated as latent truth; it is the product of those stages and inherits
their failures. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the audited pipeline.

Our primary protocol uses per-query, per-ranker min--max normalization:
\begin{equation}
\bar{x}_{q,s}(d)=
\frac{x_{q,s}(d)-\min_{d' \in D_q} x_{q,s}(d')}
     {\max_{d' \in D_q} x_{q,s}(d')-\min_{d' \in D_q} x_{q,s}(d')}.
\label{eq:minmax}
\end{equation}
Votes are then extracted from pairwise margins between normalized scores,
with ties and missing scores treated as abstentions rather than imputed
preferences. Aggregated edge weights are thresholded within one named
protocol/regime cell. The primary paper reports only the normalized
retention-matched protocol in full; raw margins and additional threshold
families are reported as robustness checks in the supplement.

Repair removes a minimum-weight feedback arc set:
\begin{equation}
F_q^\star=\arg\min_{F \subseteq E_q}
\sum_{e \in F} w_q(e)
\quad \text{s.t.}\quad (V_q,E_q \setminus F)\ \text{is acyclic}.
\label{eq:mwfas}
\end{equation}
Primary results use greedy cycle peeling; exact SCIP solves are used as a
robustness check. From the repaired or unrepaired graph, we extract graph
scores and, for hybrid methods, combine them with normalized prior scores:
\begin{equation}
h_q(d)=(1-\alpha)\hat{s}^{\mathrm{prior}}_q(d)+\alpha\hat{s}^{\mathrm{graph}}_q(d),
\label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=0.3$ in the primary analysis and a small sweep reported in
Supplement Section~B.

Table~\ref{tab:dq-taxonomy} turns the construction problem into a compact
audit taxonomy. The key decision rule is that cyclic or contradictory
structure is called a construction artifact only when a named upstream
choice is shown to create it, not merely when the pattern looks suspicious.
Raw-score scale dominance and parser-default LLM edges are examples of
measured artifacts. Residual conflict after those controls is reported as
residual conflict, not automatically as genuine disagreement.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

We use four public benchmarks: SciDocs, FiQA, HotpotQA, and BRIGHT
~\cite{cohan2020scidocs,maia2018fiqa,yang2018hotpotqa,su2024bright}. The
pipeline reuses stored BM25, TF-IDF, and MiniLM scores and fixed stored
query-ID lists. It does not regenerate upstream retrieval. Queries are
filtered only for qrels eligibility; this excludes 18 of HotpotQA's 70
stored IDs and leaves all other stored IDs usable. Candidate pools are
formed from the union of documents scored by at least one ranker and, when
needed, truncated by an RRF-centered pool policy in the primary design.
Alternative pool policies are checked in Supplement Section~D.

The original design evaluates the full within-pool order at $(P,k)=(20,20)$
for SciDocs, FiQA, and BRIGHT and $(10,10)$ for HotpotQA. To answer whether
repair changes membership when the evaluated prefix is smaller than the
available pool, we also run a larger-pool study with verified common stored
depths of 50 for SciDocs, FiQA, and BRIGHT and 35 for HotpotQA.

Primary confirmatory inference uses paired query-level nDCG deltas with
Holm correction. Bootstrap intervals, leave-one-out influence analysis,
exact repair, baseline expansion, alternative pools, and independent
protocol families are robustness checks rather than separate headline
families. MAP and MRR are secondary diagnostics only. Equivalence testing is
reported narrowly to avoid over-reading non-significance.

The compared methods include graph-free baselines (Prior, RRF, CombSUM, and
Borda), graph-only rankings (Copeland, balance, Markov, PageRank,
RankCentrality, and Bradley--Terry), and graph-plus-prior hybrids; greedy
repair is the primary intervention and exact SCIP repair is the validation
check. Table~\ref{tab:setup} summarizes the fixed dataset-specific depths
and evaluated cells.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Construction Quality Dominates the Graph}
\label{sec:construction-results}

The first finding is that raw weighted construction is not a benign
baseline. Under the raw-margin ablation, BM25 contributes a mean
conditional edge-weight share of $0.988$ whenever it participates in an
edge. Under the primary normalized protocol, that mean falls to $0.512$.
Figure~\ref{fig:bm25-share} shows that this is consistent across all four
datasets and all three vote regimes. The graph built from raw margins is
therefore largely a single-ranker graph with multi-ranker labels attached.

Normalization changes more than scale. It changes retained votes and removed
edges, and several repaired-versus-unrepaired signs flip between raw and
normalized construction. Under the normalized primary protocol, the active
\texttt{ms1} regime is highly cyclic before deleting direct mutual pairs,
but much of that cyclicity is not longer-cycle nontransitivity. Figure
~\ref{fig:cycle-decomposition} shows the decomposition. HotpotQA drops from
$63.5\%$ cyclic queries to $1.9\%$ after mutual-pair deletion, whereas FiQA
retains a larger residual ($98.3\% \rightarrow 30.8\%$). The correct report
is therefore two numbers: headline cyclicity and post-mutual residual
cyclicity.

\subsection{Repair Changes Structure More Reliably Than Retrieval}
\label{sec:repair-results}

Repair is structurally active under the normalized \texttt{ms1} graphs.
Normalized removed weight ranges from $0.029$ to $0.080$ of total graph
weight across datasets, while \texttt{ms2} is acyclic by construction.
What does not follow is a reliable retrieval benefit. The active canonical
family contains 20 \texttt{ms1} nDCG cells; the full canonical family
contains 60 cells across all three regimes. None survives Holm correction.
Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap-delta-ndcg} shows why: some point estimates are
positive, but most paired intervals still cross zero.
Table~\ref{tab:primary-findings} condenses the main empirical answers.

The larger-pool study resolves the obvious $P=k$ objection. When $P>k$,
repair does change the top-$k$ set: the mean top-$k$ membership-change rate
rises to $10.6\%$ compared with $0\%$ in the original $P=k$ design. Yet the
retrieval conclusion does not reverse. None of the 110 active larger-pool
\texttt{ms1} cells survives Holm correction, and the smallest raw p-value
still inflates to a non-significant adjusted decision. Repair can therefore
change who enters the evaluated prefix without delivering a reliable
aggregate nDCG gain.

\subsection{The Null Result Persists Under Exact Repair and Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:robustness-results}

The absence of reliable positive evidence is not a greedy-repair artifact.
Exact SCIP repair reaches proven optimality on every nonempty graph checked
($1{,}025/1{,}025$) and removes less total edge weight than greedy, yet the
retrieval decision does not change: $0/36$ canonical exact cells and $0/56$
larger-pool exact cells survive Holm correction. The paper's core contrast
is therefore sharp: improving the graph objective does not imply improving
retrieval.

The conclusion is also not limited to one convenient construction. Joint
Holm/BH families over independently-defined normalization protocols,
alternative pool policies, and added graph-ranking baselines remain null.
Simple baselines stay strong: under the primary \texttt{ms1} protocol,
CombSUM has the best dataset-macro mean nDCG ($0.554$), RRF is close
($0.546$), and the best repaired Copeland hybrid is effectively tied with
RRF ($0.546$). The manuscript therefore supports graph repair as a
measurable intervention, not as a new default fusion winner.

Power analysis clarifies what the null does and does not mean. In the
active larger-pool family, the median absolute observed repaired-minus-
unrepaired mean delta is $0.0036$, whereas the median analytic 80\% MDE is
$0.0133$ at nominal $\alpha=0.05$ and $0.0207$ after Holm correction.
Larger effects are ruled out more confidently than very small ones.
Equivalence results are correspondingly limited: only $13/110$ cells at
margin $\pm 0.005$ and $32/110$ cells at margin $\pm 0.010$ reject
non-equivalence after Holm correction. The right conclusion is no reliable
positive evidence here, not blanket practical equivalence.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results support a narrower and stronger conclusion than ``repair helps''
or ``repair never helps.'' Preference-graph repair is inseparable from graph
construction. Normalization, vote semantics, pooling, and evaluation cutoff
jointly determine the graph being repaired and the effects the evaluation
can see. In this study, raw margins create a scale-dominated graph;
normalized construction removes much of that artifact; repair remains
structurally active; and the downstream retrieval claim still fails.

Three practical implications follow. First, normalization is part of the
model, not preprocessing trivia. A weighted preference graph built from
heterogeneous raw margins can encode score scale rather than evidence.
Second, repair quality and downstream utility must be reported separately.
Exact SCIP repair improves the structural objective but does not reveal a
retrieval gain that greedy repair missed. Third, top-$k$ membership change
is a necessary but insufficient condition for a useful repair effect. The
$P>k$ study shows that observing membership changes does not by itself turn
the null into a positive result.

For practitioners, the audit logic is straightforward:
\begin{itemize}
  \item name the candidate-pool policy and check at least one alternative;
  \item normalize heterogeneous scores before interpreting weighted votes;
  \item report mutual-pair and longer-cycle cyclicity separately;
  \item test retrieval claims with paired, corrected inference rather than
    inferring them from structural activity alone.
\end{itemize}
Stored LLM judgments illustrate the same point in a different modality:
parser defaults and position bias can manufacture edges, so any future
LLM-native preference graph should be audited as a construction pipeline
before its inconsistency is interpreted.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}

The remaining boundaries are substantive, not already-resolved reviewer
concerns.

\textbf{Finite power for small effects.} The study is well positioned to
rule out larger repaired-versus-unrepaired gains, but many smaller effects
remain below the Holm-adjusted detectable scale.

\textbf{Ranker and benchmark scope.} The evidence is limited to four
benchmarks, three stored rankers, and the tested graph-construction choices.
The paper does not claim transfer to learned fusion, cross-encoder
reranking, or online evaluation.

\textbf{Candidate-pool composition remains structurally sensitive.} Pool
choice changes which documents and edges are present even though the null
retrieval conclusion is robust across the pool policies tested.

\textbf{LLM evidence is intentionally bounded.} Stored LLM judgments are used
only for protocol-quality auditing and do not independently validate the
mechanical normalized study.

\textbf{External validity is limited to tested graph-construction regimes.}
The paper does not claim that every preference-graph repair setting should
behave identically to the four benchmarks studied here.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Preference graphs in multi-ranker retrieval are derived data artifacts. The
main result of this paper is that their construction quality matters at
least as much as the repair algorithm applied afterward. Raw heterogeneous
score margins create a scale-dominated graph; normalization materially
changes retained votes, cyclicity, and repair decisions; repair then
improves structural consistency without yielding a reliable aggregate
retrieval gain under the tested settings.

That negative result is informative because it survives the checks most
likely to overturn it: larger pools with genuine $P>k$ separation, exact
SCIP repair, alternative pools, added baselines, and power-aware
interpretation. The contribution is therefore a reusable audit logic for
derived preference graphs, together with evidence that structural graph
improvement and downstream retrieval utility should be treated as separate
quality dimensions.

\section{Data Availability and Reproducibility}
\label{sec:data-availability}

\textbf{Data availability.} SciDocs, FiQA, HotpotQA, and BRIGHT are public
benchmarks and are cited from their original sources
~\cite{cohan2020scidocs,maia2018fiqa,yang2018hotpotqa,su2024bright}. This
paper redistributes no raw third-party document collection.

\textbf{Artifact availability.} The anonymous review artifact includes the
manuscript source, supplement, stored aggregate tables, protocol manifests,
hashes of score files and qrels, and the scripts used to regenerate the
reported figures and statistics. The primary evidence set is the normalized
retention-matched package; raw margins are retained only as an ablation.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} All numerical claims in the main paper trace to
stored intermediates, fixed query-ID lists, released scripts, and
machine-readable manifests. Upstream retrieval and paid APIs do not need to
be rerun for the mechanical regeneration reported here.
